Introduction

The Changing Face of Empire

Twentieth-Century International History

Gordon Martel

The international history of the twentieth century is best understood as one continuous story of imperial struggle. States — usually calling themselves "nations" — sought to impose their own version of modernity and progress on the world by force. What changed across the century was never the underlying dynamic, only the rhetoric used to disguise it: colonial empire became racial empire, then ideological empire, then economic empire — and each time, commentators mistook the new mask for the end of the story.
1900,1914,1919,1939,1955,1991,2001

1 The World in 1900: Empire as the Only Imaginable Future

Before a single shot was fired, every Great Power already understood the century in the same terms: expand or decay.

In 1900 the Great Powers were Britain, France, Russia, and the United States, and each believed it had a unique destiny that could be fulfilled only by exercising power over vast territory. A second tier of states wanted to join that club on the same terms: Germany sought to convert itself from a European power into a World Power; Japan aimed to become the premier power in Asia; Austria-Hungary clung to the hope that its multicultural structure let it act as a great power still; Italy imagined a revived Roman empire across North Africa and the eastern Mediterranean.

🟩 Concept

The "small-state" prejudice. Every major and aspiring power shared one assumption: that the age of small states had passed, that they were relics of a medieval or primeval past. This is why the pre-1914 world had no serious political alternative to empire on offer — not from statesmen, not from diplomats, not from commentators.

British leaders said this openly. Colonial secretary Joseph Chamberlain declared that the era belonged to great empires, not little states; Prime Minister Herbert Asquith went further, describing territorial expansion as a biological necessity — as natural a sign of a nation's vitality as growth is in a living body.

"The days are for great Empires and not for little States."Joseph Chamberlain, British Colonial Secretary
Territorial expansion is "as normal, as necessary, as inescapable and unmistakable a sign of vitality in a nation as the corresponding processes in the growing human body."H. H. Asquith, British Prime Minister, 1914

2 Geopolitics: The Pseudo-Science of Empire

Empire needed a theory. Geopolitics supplied one — and immediately ran into a problem.

This era produced "geopolitics" as a discipline, alongside its intellectual siblings anthropology, sociology, and eugenics — all handmaidens to imperial justification. Its account of the future was simple: the future belonged to Russia and the United States, continental "heartland" powers whose interior resources and populations were immune from naval attack and connected by rail.

Mackinder — geopolitics' founding figure — then had to explain why Britain, an island without a "heartland," could still count among the great. His answer: empire was Britain's substitute geography. Ships could replace railways; Canadian prairies, African minerals, and Indian manpower could replace the natural resource base that Russia and America enjoyed at home.

Germany's Dilemma

Geopolitics left an awkward question for everyone else: Germany, France, Austria-Hungary, Italy, Japan, Turkey, even Serbia. In the vocabulary of geopolitics they were merely "the periphery"; in the vocabulary of eugenics, "the decadent." German chancellor Prince Bernhard von Bülow captured the anxiety this produced in 1904, asking whether twentieth-century Germany would be the hammer or the anvil of world politics.

Kaiser Wilhelm II refused to choose between the two options: an imperial centre extending south and east would be paired with a colonial periphery — bases from which to threaten the older empires in Africa, Asia, and even the Americas. In this design, the 1879 alliance with Austria-Hungary was essential.

3 Alliances as Instruments of Empire

The alliance system did not cause the war for empire — it was empire's tool, built in advance for exactly this purpose.

The Habsburgs could keep the fruits of empire only by playing second fiddle to the Hohenzollerns. Wilhelm II made the hierarchy explicit when he thanked Franz Josef for acting as his "brilliant second" during the Moroccan crisis — a crisis Germany had manufactured to show France that its empire depended on German goodwill. Austria-Hungary accepted the subordinate role because Berlin's backing was what let Vienna and Budapest continue to prosper despite the empire's discordant internal nationalities.

France, by contrast, refused the second-fiddle role Germany assigned it — having already accepted second fiddle to Britain when it gave up its Egyptian ambitions in the 1904 Entente Cordiale. When the French prime minister wavered under German pressure, British foreign secretary Sir Edward Grey conjured the British Expeditionary Force into being to stiffen French resolve. This is how Britain's "continental commitment" was born — not from sentiment, but to preserve the British Empire.

4 How the First World War Actually Began

Martel rejects four familiar explanations in favour of one: imperial collision in the Balkans.

Instead, war broke out because south-eastern Europe was the one place where the imperial fears of Austria-Hungary and Russia collided — the one kind of issue guaranteed to bring each power's allies to its side. The Habsburg empire moved to crush the Serb/Slav threat to its imperial integrity; the Romanov empire moved to shield its Serbian client from destruction. The alliances, the armaments, the diplomacy, and the investments were not the source of the war — they were instruments, built in advance to serve imperial assumptions that already existed.

the same imperial logic reached beyond the great powers

5 The Lesser Powers' Imperial Wars

Italy, Japan, and Turkey did not fight for ideals. They fought — or waited — for empire.

Italy and Romania, both formally bound to the Central Powers, declared their treaty obligations void and waited on the sidelines for whichever side would meet their territorial demands. Japan joined the Entente specifically to seize German possessions in the Far East. Turkey joined the Central Powers to realise the ambitions of the Young Turks: rejecting the sultan's cosmopolitan court and Islam as imperial foundations, the modernising Constantinople regime invented a secular Pan-Turanianism — a project to unify all Turkic-speaking peoples, recover recently lost territory (including Turkic populations inside Russian Azerbaijan and Persia), and eventually build an empire stretching from the Balkans to China.

The Italian Case

Martel treats Italy as the clearest proof that the war was fought for empire, not ideals: Rome produced none of the rhetoric of "making the world safe for democracy." Italy held back in July 1914 and waited for the highest territorial bid — sealed, quite literally, in agreements that included £50 million in cash. Woodrow Wilson tried to deny Italy its promised gains by denouncing "secret agreements, secretly arrived at" — but the Italians, who lost over 600,000 dead for those promises, refused to give them up, and turned to fascism when they felt cheated at the 1919 Paris settlement.

6 Against the "War for Nothing" Myth

If the war wasn't fought for civilisation or ideology, popular memory needed a villain instead. Martel argues this is a comforting evasion.

The standard narrative treats the First World War as an Anglo-German confrontation fought, tragically, for nothing — no clash of civilisations, no ideological contest — and therefore explains it as the work of wicked men: militarists, arms manufacturers, financiers, and complicit politicians. Martel calls this comforting story nearly unthinkable to challenge, yet argues that most contemporaries understood perfectly well that their national destiny — empire — was what hung in the balance, and were prepared to fight for it.

7 Empire as the War's Real Winner

Contrary to a common assumption, the war did not expose empire's weakness — it proved its strength.

No one believed empire was dead after 1918, and few believed the League of Nations' "mandate" system was more than camouflage for the fact that the Entente now controlled practically all of Africa, the Middle East, and the Pacific. Historians who read the Boer War as evidence of imperial weakness, Martel argues, read it wrong — the following decade showed the opposite, as South Africans, Anzacs, Canadians, Sikhs, and Gurkhas all marched loyally to war. While Germany and Austria-Hungary were slowly starved of resources between 1914–18, empire gave the Allies the material bounty of the entire world — men, metals, and food.

🟩 Concept

The lesson of 1914–18: war in the twentieth century was about empire — fought to keep it or expand it, won by those who had it, lost by those who didn't. This is the pivot on which Martel's entire chapter turns, and it recurs, restated, at every later stage of the century.

but victory produced an unexpected paradox at home

8 The Interwar Paradox: Idealism amid Renewed Imperialism

The postwar world was drenched in idealist language — yet imperialism, far from being discredited, was being rebuilt in bolder form.

Paradoxically, the postwar world was infused with idealism, driven by the competing ideals of the losers and of winners who felt like losers. The only realists left — Britain and France — stood no chance in the ideologically charged world they had themselves helped create, since pragmatism inspires no one.

Revisionist success looked unlikely at first: Russia was in civil war, Germany in disarray, Italy near revolt, Japan beset by rioting. Yet within a decade, each revisionist power built new regimes on "modernist" philosophies of race and class, using "futurist" tools — mass communication, propaganda, surveillance, control — to pursue radical new imperial designs, bolder and more aggressive than before. Meanwhile the older empires of Britain and France, still willing to suppress native revolts in India, Iraq, Syria, and Algeria exactly as in the previous century, had lost self-confidence at home.

9 Cracks in the British and French Empires

The war that saved the empire also broke the psychological contract that had sustained it.

The Entente empires had been built cheaply — through minuscule military forces commanded by aristocrats out of step with modernising liberal regimes at home. The Great War changed that bargain completely. Britain's most popular wartime slogan was "Save the Empire," with propaganda casting the Kaiser as bent on its destruction — but if that was the stakes, the price proved unimaginable: nearly a million dead, a near-bankrupt treasury.

Having paid that price, the empire itself began demanding change: less imperial control from London, more self-government. The "white dominions" received independent representation in the League of Nations and mandates of their own in Southwest Africa and the Pacific; the 1931 Statute of Westminster confirmed their drift toward autonomy. Ireland's demand for independence grew violent from Easter 1916 onward, despite the loyalty of the Irish Volunteers during the war itself. India, despite its wartime contribution, saw Gandhi launch his first non-cooperation campaign. The new "empire" carved out at Versailles — Palestine, Iraq — required unprecedented policing.

By the 1930s, an emotional equation had formed between the sacrifice of the trenches and the persistence of empire: a generation had died for what — the Irish Free State? Indian dominion status? A Zionist settlement in Palestine? The only justification left for this "Third British Empire" was that it served "the natives" by setting them on a path to democracy — a rhetorical promise with a fatal flaw once colonial subjects began demanding that London actually honour it.

10 The Racial Empires: Germany and Japan

A new justification for empire emerged that Britain and France never used: the claim of racial superiority itself.

Where Britain and France professed to rule in the interest of the ruled, the imperial ethos of Germany and Japan was explicitly racist — a claimed right to command "lesser" beings by virtue of racial superiority. Martel argues that comparing German and Japanese fascism as parallel "meanings" misses the point: both shared the same racist core, one dressed in modern pseudo-science, the other in mystical mytho-history.

In Germany, the distinction between "nation" and "race" was not understood at first — not even by German Jews, who tried to assert their German-ness and patriotism, only to discover that their religion was never the issue: their blood was. Koreans learned the equivalent lesson first, at Japanese hands. The two most brutal theatres of the Second World War were exactly where these racial ideologies were most absolute: the Pacific, where the purity of the "Yamato soul" was meant to let "one hundred million hearts beat as one," and eastern Europe, where Nazi ideas of the enemy as Untermenschen dominated.

11 The American Empire That Dared Not Speak Its Name

The "natural" empires needed no racial claim — the US and USSR only had to hold onto what geopoliticians said was already theirs.

Interwar American "revisionism" — popular enough to be written into neutrality legislation — held that the US had entered the First World War for empire: that followers of naval strategist Alfred Thayer Mahan had combined with J. P. Morgan & Co. to argue that trade follows the flag and a two-ocean navy was essential to a flourishing free-enterprise economy.

US President, 1901–09
Theodore Roosevelt

The era's iconic imperialist: led the San Juan Hill charge, fought to retain the Philippines after the Spanish–American War, and framed America's continental expansion as the completion of "manifest destiny" in The Winning of the West.

US President, 1913–21
Woodrow Wilson

Believed that with the frontier "won," America needed a substitute — conquering world markets via what he called "the imperial president." Intervened in Mexico, the Dominican Republic, and Haiti.

Martel's inversion here is deliberate: it was not Wilson and the Americans who were naïve at the 1919 Paris conference, but the Europeans — who failed to reckon with what US occupation of Cuba and the Philippines, or Wilsonian intervention in Mexico, the Dominican Republic, and Haiti, already revealed about American imperial practice.

12 The Soviet Alternative: Empire of Class

Wilson's greatest fear was a rival imperial system built not on trade, nation, or race — but on class.

The Bolshevik model cut through both old European imperialism and the new American version by grounding itself in class rather than trade or nation. The Comintern's creation, and the proclamations of Lenin, Trotsky, and Bukharin for world revolution, left no doubt that expanding the system was seen as essential to its own survival.

Lenin had learned from the war that imperialism was "the highest stage of capitalism" — so, by definition, the new Soviet state could not itself be imperialist. The peace taught him a further lesson: that western capitalists were capable of uniting to carve up the world by mutual agreement, which he took to be the League of Nations' real purpose. The Soviet alternative, then, was proletarians and peasants "freely" combining into a new socialist federation — but the Politburo, commissars, and OGPU quickly imposed, across the former tsarist empire, a centralised authority that any tsarist administrator would have envied.

When Nazi Germany grew aggressive, the USSR briefly treated the League as legitimate and proposed a popular front against fascism — a gambit that ended with the Nazi–Soviet pact. Apologists blame British and French appeasement for forcing Stalin's hand; Martel argues Stalin was instead drawn by the chance to expand Soviet territory into Poland, Finland, and the Baltic states.

13 The Second World War and the Empires That Survived

The war destroyed the racial empires of Germany and Japan — but imperialism itself survived, fully intact.

Even while the war was being fought, the Allies recommitted themselves to empire. Stalin and the Politburo were convinced that future Soviet security required extending Soviet-controlled territory and exercising what imperial Britain had once called "paramountcy" over bordering states. Churchill and De Gaulle were equally convinced that Britain's and France's futures depended on retaining their empires.

14 The Cold War as Disguised Imperialism

Empire did not end in 1945 — it learned, for the first time, to deny its own name.

Franklin Roosevelt revived Wilson's imperial vision. His plan abandoned American isolationism outright — an isolationism whose impracticality Pearl Harbor demonstrated beyond argument — and relocated the reconstituted League (now the United Nations) from neutral Geneva to New York, the metropolis of the era's greatest power. FDR believed the League had failed by not being imperial enough; his answer was "four policemen" — the US, USSR, Britain, and China — each responsible for order within its own sphere.

🟩 Concept

The Four Policemen. Had it been fully realised, this would have been the largest scheme in history for peacefully dividing the globe among empires. It failed for two concrete reasons: the Chinese revolution, and mutual suspicion among the European Allies. Four empires partitioning the world peacefully instead collapsed into a bipolar struggle between two of them.

Both the US and USSR then did something new: each denied its own imperial character while accusing the other of being imperialism's very essence. Imperialism became, in Martel's words, "the policy that dare not speak its name" — manipulation of foreign politics, aid packages, strategic support, even military intervention were justified as serving others' interests, never as a great power's right. As the physicist-turned-novelist C. P. Snow observed, being told something was "in your own best interests" usually meant someone was about to suffer for it.

15 The Invention of the Third World

A new term, coined at Bandung in 1955, offered postcolonial states a way to avoid choosing sides — and a new set of leaders who rebuilt empire under different names.

It was in the context of American and Soviet mutual denial that the "Third World" was invented at the 1955 Bandung Conference. The term — later devalued into a synonym for "where the poor live" — originally proclaimed a refusal to choose between the two imperial colossi. New postcolonial elites fluent in anti-colonial rhetoric quickly discovered they could exploit the US–Soviet rivalry for their own purposes, purposes that rarely served their populations as a whole.

Martel treats both Pan-Arabism and Pan-Africanism (the latter promoted by Ghana's Kwame Nkrumah on the same logic) as new, improved versions of imperial design rather than a genuine alternative to empire: an assumption that small postcolonial states could survive only by acquiring a new imperial framework to replace the one they had just shed. Both projects died with the leaders who dreamed them.

while new states were inventing empire, old Europe was quietly abandoning it — or reinventing it

16 Old Europe's Reinvention: From Property to Profit

Decolonization looked slow at the time. In hindsight, Martel calls it electrical — and it produced two very different responses.

The first states to change were not the great powers but the small ones. Belgium and the Netherlands, having lived through the horrors of the Congo and the Aceh War, abandoned any imperial future and imagined a new past instead; "Benelux" — initially an unremarkable trading arrangement — took shape just as Suez and Algeria were ending empire for Britain and France as they had known it.

Colonies "fell away or hacked themselves off" within a few short years. France resisted more violently than Britain, but both met resistance even from former collaborators, while publics at home stopped caring about empire altogether. Yet — paradoxically — as the empire of property shriveled, an older, mid-Victorian ideal of empire revived to replace it: why bother colonizing, occupying, administering, and policing people who demand freedom, when metals and oil will still flow to Europe and pay a healthy margin to the head offices in London and Paris?

17 End of History? The Post-Cold War Illusion

When the Berlin Wall fell, some declared the imperial story over for good. Martel treats this as another premature epitaph.

When the physical Berlin Wall and the metaphorical Iron Curtain came down, some argued this marked the "end of history" — a future no longer contested, since all states would converge on free-market economics sustained by democratic politics, the twentieth century having shown that no rival system (property empire, race empire, ideological empire) actually worked.

18 9/11 and the Return of Contested Empire

The neo-imperial daydream of a unified post-Cold War world ended on a single morning in September 2001.

The September 11, 2001 attack on the World Trade Center shattered the "end of history" thesis. It showed that the imperial metropolis itself was vulnerable to a small number of determined attackers, and the "War on Terror" that followed showed history had not ended and the world was not, in fact, one. "Islamic Fundamentalism" emerged as a rhetorical catch-all — used by proponents and critics alike — to describe a way of life supposedly incompatible with Western liberalism, a division no one a century earlier had expected religion, rather than race, nation, or class, to define.

🟩 Concept

Closing the loop. Martel ends where he began: the world remains divided by competing visions of empire. Only the vocabulary changes — colonies, then race, then ideology, then civilizational/religious difference — while "the underlying dynamics of the imperial process" (his opening phrase) persist unaltered.

§ End-of-Chapter Revision

Chapter Summary

Martel argues that twentieth-century international history is a single, continuous story of imperial struggle, not a series of unrelated conflicts. In 1900, no serious alternative to empire existed; the Great Powers and their aspirants (Germany, Japan, Austria-Hungary, Italy) all pursued expansion as a condition of national survival, underpinned by the pseudo-science of geopolitics. The First World War was not caused by arms races, alliances, or miscalculation, but by an imperial collision between Austria-Hungary and Russia over the Balkans — a war whose lesser participants (Italy, Japan, Turkey) fought for explicitly imperial stakes. Empire survived the war strengthened, not weakened, even as the moral cost of victory eroded Britain's and France's confidence in their own empires at home. The interwar period saw revisionist powers rebuild empire on radical new bases — race (Germany, Japan), class (USSR), and market power ("naturally," the US) — producing a Second World War that destroyed the racial empires specifically while leaving imperialism itself intact. The Cold War displaced imperial competition into a rhetoric of mutual denial between the US and USSR, giving rise to the "Third World" (Bandung, 1955) and postcolonial imperial substitutes like Pan-Arabism and Pan-Africanism. Old Europe's small states abandoned empire while its great powers reinvented it as commercial influence rather than direct rule. The "end of history" declared after 1991 proved premature, and the attacks of September 11, 2001 confirmed that empire's underlying dynamics persist — now expressed through a rhetoric of civilizational and religious difference.

🟨 Exam Tip

Note the rhetorical pattern Martel identifies: powers who already had empire explained why holding one was natural and good; powers without one explained why lacking one was unnatural and bad. This asymmetry of justification is a strong exam point.

🟦 Definition

Geopolitics — a pseudo-scientific discipline, associated above all with Halford Mackinder, that explained national power as a function of geography: control of a secure interior "heartland," connected by rail/river to resources and population, as opposed to a maritime periphery.

🟨 Exam Tip

Mackinder invented a science of geographic destiny, then carved a self-serving exception into it for his own country (ships as Britain's "substitute" heartland). Watch for this pattern whenever a power constructs a theory of why it deserves greatness.

🟦 Definition

Drang nach Osten ("drive to the east") — German strategy of extending power south/east through continental expansion rather than overseas colonies; symbolised by the Berlin–Baghdad railway.

🟥 Common Mistake

Do not explain 1914 as caused by "mounting tensions," an "escalating arms race," "the alliance system," or diplomatic miscalculation. Martel explicitly rejects all four. The Moroccan crises (1905, 1911), Bosnian annexation (1908), and Libyan/Balkan wars (1912–13) are misleading parallels — in none did the Great Powers come close to war.

🟦 Definition

Greater Serbia — the Serbian imperial project that precipitated the July Crisis: not a nation-state, but an empire intended to absorb Croatians, Montenegrins, Bosnians, and any Balkan people the Serbs judged to lack "national consciousness."

🟦 Definition

Pan-Turanianism — secular imperial ideology invented by the Young Turk regime, aiming to unite all Turkic-speaking peoples ("Turan") under Ottoman/Turkish leadership, replacing Islam and the sultanate as organising principle.

🟨 Exam Tip

Italy (600,000+ dead) and Japan (~300 dead) both came away aggrieved for the same underlying reason despite wildly different costs: both looked at the swollen British/French empires and concluded the war enlarged them, not the world. Use this pairing to show imperial grievance — not casualty count — drove interwar revisionism.

⭐ High-Yield Takeaway

The "war fought for nothing, caused by wicked men" narrative is, in Martel's account, a retrospective myth letting participants avoid admitting the war was a rational imperial contest. Italy's frank, cash-denominated bargaining is the clearest counter-evidence.

🟨 Exam Tip

"Idealism" and "imperialism" were not opposites in this period; they ran in parallel. The war rhetorically discredited old-style imperialism while the practical machinery of empire (mandates, occupation, policing) actually expanded.

🟦 Definition

Statute of Westminster (1931) — British legislation granting the self-governing dominions (Canada, Australia, South Africa, etc.) full legislative independence from the UK Parliament.

🟥 Common Mistake

Britain/France's legitimacy dilemma (promised tutelage toward freedom, then resisted delivering it) was not shared by the racial empires of Germany/Japan or the ideological empires of USSR/US — each justified rule on entirely different, non-promissory grounds.

🟦 Definition

Untermenschen — Nazi racial category denoting peoples (chiefly Slavic and Jewish populations of eastern Europe) deemed racially "sub-human," used to justify occupation, exploitation, and extermination.

🟦 Definition

Yamato minzoku — the Japanese imperial concept of the "Yamato race/nation" as a uniquely homogeneous people descended from a divine sovereign, used to justify leadership of the "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere."

🟦 Definition

Monroe Doctrine (Wilsonian use) — the US claim to a right of continual intervention in Latin American affairs, which Wilson insisted would continue regardless of the League, and which was itself written into the League's Covenant.

🟨 Exam Tip

Wilson's system was "a brilliantly designed imperial system for the twentieth century" — but Americans weren't ready to embrace it until 1945. Republicans/Midwesterners in 1919 were horrified a League could send American soldiers abroad. This explains the gap between Wilson's 1919 vision and its later realisation under FDR.

🟦 Definition

Comintern (Communist International) — Soviet-directed organisation coordinating world communist parties toward international revolution; the institutional expression of a class-based imperial project.

🟥 Common Mistake

Popular memory assumes WWII was fought to preserve democracy/rescue Jews. Martel's sharper claim: the Anglo-French Entente, backed by the US, would have tolerated Nazi/Japanese authoritarianism and turned a blind eye to Jews/Koreans had the Axis not threatened British, French, and American empires.

🟨 Exam Tip

"Saving socialism from capitalist imperialism" (Soviet) and "defending democracy from the Evil Empire" (American) turned every uncommitted place on earth into contested terrain — the clearest illustration of Martel's thesis that only vocabulary, not dynamics, changed.

🟦 Definition

Third World (original sense) — coined at the 1955 Bandung Conference to describe states refusing to align with either the American or Soviet bloc — not, originally, a synonym for poverty.

⭐ High-Yield Takeaway

Old empires of property were discovered to be both unsustainable and unnecessary — new empires of profit and play could take their place just as effectively, without the cost of direct administration.

🟥 Common Mistake

"End of history" talk was, per Martel, little more than American triumphalism. Rwanda, Kosovo, and Mogadishu already contradicted it before it was fully articulated — dismissed at the time as mere echoes of a fading colonial past.

Four Faces of Empire: Property → Race → Ideology → Civilization/religion. Same imperial dynamic, four successive disguises.
