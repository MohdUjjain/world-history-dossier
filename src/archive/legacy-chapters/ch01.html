Chapter One

Imperialism

The Rise and Dissolution of the Formal Empires

John MacKenzie

The twentieth century opened with European and American empires at their territorial peak and closed with formal decolonization all but complete — yet the century's defining conflicts, borders, and institutions were themselves imperial creations. Empire did not simply end: it left behind the artificial boundaries, ethnic frictions, and migration patterns that continue to generate conflict, while its regulatory functions passed quietly to a new set of international institutions.
1900,1910,1919,1931,1945,1960,1994,1997

1 Empires in the Early Twentieth Century

By 1900 the map of formal empire was nearly finished being drawn. What remained was administering it — and discovering how unstable "finished" really was.

By 1900 most major acts of imperial acquisition had already occurred. The late nineteenth century had seen the near-total partition of Africa and a scramble for Pacific islands among the British, Germans, French, and Americans. The United States, already an imperial power through its continental expansion and the annexations of Alaska and Hawaii, expanded further overseas after the 1898 Spanish–American War, acquiring the Philippines and Caribbean territory from a declining Spain. Russia consolidated its Central and East Asian holdings through railway building, including the Trans-Siberian line. Older rivalries persisted: British and French forces confronted each other at Fashoda in 1898, and Italy suffered a major setback when defeated by Ethiopian forces at the Battle of Adowa in 1896 — a defeat fascist Italy would later be determined to avenge.

Formal Empire, Informal Empire, Protectorate

🟩 Concept

The bourgeois intermediary class. Imperial rule depended on a layer of indigenous rulers, commercial collaborators, and westernized elites trained in Western ideas — and it was precisely from this bourgeois intermediary class that twentieth-century nationalism arose. This point explains where the leaders of the later decolonization movements (§7) actually came from.

Zones from Ottoman Decline

Ottoman weakness — following its sixteenth- to eighteenth-century peak — had already reshaped Greece and the Balkans in the nineteenth century and cost it Cyprus. British control of Egypt remained nominal until a protectorate was declared in 1914; Algeria and Tunisia were under formal French control, Morocco fell into France's informal sphere after German-instigated crises, and Libya became Italian after a brief 1911–12 war.

The Americas

European empires held formal control across almost the entire Caribbean and parts of Central/South America's coasts. American influence, anchored in the 1823 Monroe Doctrine, treated South America as its own sphere even where Britain held real economic power (notably in Argentina). Britain's Falkland Islands (Malvinas) — a source of the 1982 Anglo-Argentine War — and other Atlantic islands remained under formal British control.

Sub-Saharan Africa and Asia

Sub-Saharan Africa was almost entirely divided among the Portuguese, British, French, German, Italian, and Belgian empires; only Ethiopia remained fully independent. In Asia, Britain held territory from Yemen and the Gulf through India to Ceylon, Burma, the Malay states, and Singapore; Hong Kong grew as a commercial hub after China ceded the New Territories in 1898 on a 99-year lease. Japan had acquired Formosa (Taiwan) in 1895 and added Korea in 1910. France held Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos; the Dutch held the East Indies; Siam (Thailand) remained under informal, mainly British, influence.

2 The Unique Power of the British Empire

"The British Empire" is a misleading singular — in practice, Britain ran several distinct empires simultaneously.

MacKenzie identifies multiple, structurally distinct "British empires" operating side by side, from India's mix of direct rule and princely states, through the self-governing white settlement colonies, to a global network of strategic islands and dependent colonies administered directly by the Crown.

3 Loss of Equilibrium in the International Imperial Order

Britain held the largest empire the world had ever seen — and was losing its grip on it in real time.

Britain was a "weary Titan, staggering under the too great orb of its fate."Joseph Chamberlain, Colonial Secretary

Despite ruling "the empire on which the sun never set," Britain was being overtaken in steel production by both the US and Germany, and its cherished "two-power naval standard" was becoming unsustainable. Domestic strains (Irish nationalism, labor unrest) compounded the anxiety.

The Anglo-Boer War (1899–1902)

The clearest challenge came from Chamberlain and High Commissioner Alfred Milner's drive to dominate southern Africa. Britain, largely provoking the war, aimed to control Transvaal gold, secure political rights for European migrants in the Boer republics, and protect the Cape and Natal from foreign alliance risk.

Realignment: Japan, Germany, and the Ententes

Recognizing it could no longer maintain a Far Eastern naval/military presence alone, Britain abandoned "splendid isolation" and allied with Japan in 1902 — a rising power Britain saw as structurally similar to itself. Germany's naval building program, ordered once the Kaiser identified naval power as the true source of British predominance, alarmed the Admiralty and triggered a shift toward concentrated dreadnought battleships (first launched 1906, under Admiral Jack Fisher's influence).

The perceived German threat drove Britain into new ententes: with France in 1904 (ending long imperial friction), and with traditional rival Russia soon after. Anglo-Russian tension had run along three fronts — Russian pressure on the Ottoman Black Sea/Dardanelles routes, Russian encroachment on Persia, Afghanistan, and India's Northwest Frontier (the "Great Game"), and Russian expansion threatening European treaty-port privileges in China.

Russia's threat in the Far East was neutralized by Japan's decisive victory in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–5), climaxing at Tsushima (1905) — a battle Japanese Admiral Togo explicitly likened to Trafalgar a century earlier. The resulting Anglo-Russian entente divided Persia into spheres of influence: Russia in the north, Britain in the south, protecting its Gulf commercial and strategic zone.

🟩 Concept

The technological infrastructure of empire. British dominance rested on globally dominant shipping, river steamers on the Euphrates, Irrawaddy, Nile, Niger, Congo, and Amazon, submarine telegraph cables enabling near-instant communication, and expanding rail networks — a system increasingly, if unevenly, being adopted by non-European peoples too.

Beneath this apparent strength ran real anxiety: the Maji Maji revolt (German Tanganyika) and the Herero revolt (German Southwest Africa) were both crushed with great brutality; Britain faced Sayyid Abdullah Hassan's rebellion in Somaliland; and nationalist movements were gathering strength in India, South Africa, and West Africa among educated elites. On the eve of war, Britain and Germany even confirmed an agreement to partition the Portuguese Empire in Africa.

4 Imperialism and the First World War

Fought for European reasons or not, the war's battles and its settlement redrew the imperial map completely.

In Africa, South African, British, and Belgian forces captured German Southwest Africa and Tanganyika, and Germany lost Togo and Cameroon in the west; General von Lettow-Vorbeck sustained a German guerrilla campaign in the east that lasted the entire war. German Pacific possessions were also captured.

South African General/Statesman
Jan Smuts

His African campaigns brought him a seat in the British Imperial War Cabinet, the rank of field marshal, and major influence at Versailles and in founding the League of Nations.

British Officer
T. E. Lawrence

With Sharif Husayn, Emir of the Hijaz, fomented the Arab Revolt against Ottoman rule as British and Indian forces advanced through Mesopotamia.

Ottoman entry into the war on Germany's side — influenced heavily by German investment such as the Baghdad railway — led directly to Ottoman destruction. Wartime diplomacy relied heavily on secret agreements: Italy joined the Allies in 1916 for promised territorial rewards, while Britain and France agreed on dividing Middle Eastern spoils and debated a Jewish homeland in the region.

India's Interwar Turbulence Begins

A brief Afghan war broke out in 1919. The Indian National Congress (founded 1885) had already split into militant and constitutional wings; wartime "loyalty" saw Gandhi (soon "Mahatma") serve in an ambulance corps. British attempts to mix constitutional reform with repression produced the Jallianwala Bagh (Amritsar) massacre, killing roughly 400 unarmed demonstrators. Gandhi built control over the nationalist movement via passive resistance combined with constitutional negotiation — but the separatist Muslim League (founded 1905) grew as a rival power base, and by 1940, under Mohammed Ali Jinnah, was demanding a separate Muslim state.

5 The Interwar Years: Golden Age and Fault Lines

Empire in the 1920s and '30s looks, paradoxically, both more polished than ever and already cracking.

Administrations became more systematic; officials were more carefully trained. Economic hardship drove governments to actively cultivate imperial sentiment: major exhibitions at Wembley (1924–5), Glasgow (1938), Paris (1925, 1931); Britain's Empire Marketing Board (1926); and, after years of debate, imperial trade preference at the 1932 Ottawa agreements. American immigration quotas redirected European migration toward the empires instead, supplemented by British ex-servicemen's settlement schemes.

Constitutional Change

1922 treaties restored effective independence to Ireland (minus six northern counties) and Egypt (Britain retaining defense/foreign affairs to protect the Suez Canal, formalized further in 1936); similar arrangements followed in Mesopotamia (Iraq) and Transjordan. The Americans pursued the same informal-control logic, granting self-government to Cuba (1934) and the Philippines (1935) while retaining Puerto Rico.

Depression-Era Strain

Cyclical depressions — the early 1920s downturn and the far more severe post-1929 crisis — drove falling wages and living standards across imperial systems (South Africa, cushioned by gold production, was a partial exception). Responses diverged sharply by empire.

The League of Nations proved equally powerless against Japanese aggression: Manchuria was invaded in 1931 and turned into the puppet state of Manchukuo, followed by incursions into China proper. With the Anglo-Japanese alliance unrenewed since WWI, Britain now saw Japan as a direct threat and built extensive fortifications at Singapore.

6 The Second World War: A Different Kind of Imperial War

Where the First World War's imperial battles were fought in sub-Saharan Africa, the Second's were fought in Asia, the Pacific, and North Africa — and exposed the European empires as hollow.

Japan and Italy, Allied powers in the First World War, realigned with Germany in the Second. Japan's rapid conquest of European colonies across the Far East and Southeast Asia in 1941–2 — using an alliance with Thailand to strike into Burma, reaching toward British India itself, while also threatening Australia and New Zealand from the Pacific — demonstrated the European empires' extreme military weakness in the region.

🟩 Concept

A divided French empire. French territorial allegiance split between Vichy (collaborationist) and Free French forces: French North Africa and (initially) French West Africa followed Vichy, while French Equatorial Africa backed de Gaulle's Free French. The split reached as far as the tiny islands of St. Pierre and Miquelon off Canada, seized from Vichy control by the Free French with Churchill's backing.

The Mediterranean theatre (Vichy North Africa plus Italian Libya) drew British, then American, forces via Egypt. Japanese occupation across Asia — by displacing European rule — helped incubate communist resistance movements in Indochina, Malaya, and Indonesia. After the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki ended the war, the Americans concluded that the western empires in Asia needed to go, fearing their weakness would otherwise let communism spread, as it had in North Korea after Japan's withdrawal.

The Dutch, weakened by wartime occupation, cannot hold Indonesia — independence follows Sukarno's insurrection.

In Malaya, by contrast, Britain defeated a mainly ethnic-Chinese communist insurgency through fortified villages and a "hearts and minds" strategy — helped considerably by Britain's plan to hand power to indigenous Malays who had little sympathy for the insurgents.

7 Decolonization

No one in 1945 predicted the European empires would be almost entirely dismantled within twenty-five years. MacKenzie tracks how the "colonial logjam" broke.

Initially, both France and Portugal tried to bind their colonies more tightly to the metropole, and Britain and France launched major colonial investment programs. League mandates transferred to UN trusteeship. Yet the logjam was already moving.

India: The Departure That Set the Pattern

India had become increasingly ungovernable through the interwar period and the war. The 1935 Government of India Act introduced "dyarchy" — partial devolution of domestic power to Indian provinces while Britain retained defense and foreign affairs centrally.

Viceroy Lord Linlithgow's unilateral 1939 declaration of war on India's behalf — legal, but deeply undiplomatic — badly damaged British standing. Unlike in WWI, Indian loyalty fractured: the 1941 Quit India movement led to Gandhi's imprisonment, while the Muslim League gained ground precisely as Congress was disabled by repression. Postwar Britain, crushed by debt, could not sustain its presence. Attlee's Labour government resolved on rapid independence; Mountbatten, sent to execute it, concluded partition was unavoidable.

The Middle East and Palestine

Britain granted effective independence to Mesopotamia (Iraq) and Transjordan. In Palestine, rising Jewish immigration fleeing Nazi persecution collided with British efforts to restrict it; pressure from Jewish paramilitary groups including the Irgun and Stern Gang made the situation ungovernable, and Britain withdrew. The ensuing conflict between Jewish settlers and Arab populations established the state of Israel in 1948.

Southeast Asia and Africa: Federation as Strategy

Britain often pursued federation as a decolonization strategy — in Malaya (federated from 1896, independent 1957) and in Central Africa (the 1953 Central African Federation of the Rhodesias and Nyasaland). The Malayan federation unraveled when Singapore's mostly Chinese population unbalanced the new state's demographics, leading to Singapore's 1965 secession. The Central African Federation, seen by Africans as a vehicle for maintaining white power, collapsed after 1959, splitting into independent Zambia and Malawi.

Britain granted West Africa earlier self-government — Ghana from 1951, independent 1957, followed swiftly by Nigeria, Sierra Leone, and the Gambia. France instead tried tightening French West and Equatorial Africa into the postwar Fourth Republic's structure, before de Gaulle's new Fifth Republic reversed course and granted rapid independence in 1960. Algeria, with its large settler population, proved a far harder case, achieving independence only in 1962 after conflict verging on civil war.

Southern Africa's Long Endgame

Britain granted independence to Bechuanaland, Basutoland, and Swaziland specifically to prevent their absorption by an expansionist South Africa. Rhodesia's white minority under Ian Smith unilaterally declared independence in 1965, surviving until 1980 largely through the backing of the apartheid regime that had governed South Africa since 1948. South Africa's own "Bantustan" system received no international recognition except from Israel. Real change arrived only in the 1990s: Namibian independence (1990), Nelson Mandela's release, and the negotiated end of apartheid by 1994 — closely paralleled by the Soviet Union's own imperial dissolution from 1989 onward.

the chapter closes by asking what, if anything, replaced formal empire

What Replaced Empire?

MacKenzie ends by noting that regulatory functions once held directly by imperial powers passed to new international institutions — the World Bank, the IMF, the "Group of Eight" — while some argue the United States and China now perform quasi-imperial roles in place of a declined Russia. The world, he suggests, remains divided as it was in imperial times: into the powerful and rich versus the weak and poor, visible in stark global inequalities of consumption, education, and health.

§ End-of-Chapter Revision

Chapter Summary

MacKenzie traces formal empire from its near-complete territorial peak around 1900 through to its near-complete dissolution by century's end. The early twentieth century was a world already carved into formal and informal zones of control, with Britain's empire uniquely composed of several distinct systems. British global predominance was already fraying before 1914 — exposed by the costly Boer War, naval competition with Germany, and a reluctant abandonment of "splendid isolation" via ententes with Japan, France, and Russia. The First World War, whatever its European origins, produced a decisively imperial settlement: new mandates, and even former colonies becoming mandate-holding powers. The interwar years combined an intensified imperial culture with mounting internal contradictions, especially in India, while Depression-era pressure pushed empires toward tighter control or reluctant reform. The Second World War, fought principally in Asia/Pacific and North Africa, exposed the European empires' military weakness and catalyzed the nationalist and communist movements that would dismantle them. Decolonization then proceeded unevenly but rapidly from 1947 through the 1960s "decade of decolonization" in Africa, reaching its final acts with the end of apartheid (1994) and the transfer of Hong Kong (1997). Empire's formal end left behind a nation-state order still shaped by imperial borders, migrations, and inequalities.

🟦 Definition

Formal empire — the imperial power directly administers territory, building infrastructure and ports to serve international trade.

🟦 Definition

Informal empire — the imperial power exercises economic influence while permitting indigenous (if weak) local rule.

🟦 Definition

Protectorate — a British designation for a territory whose boundaries the imperial power set, while maintaining an appearance of traditional local rule, overseen by the Foreign Office.

🟨 Exam Tip

Note the role of chartered companies in extending British rule at minimal state cost: the Royal Niger Company (wound up 1898), the British South Africa Company (ruled the Rhodesias until 1923), and the British North Borneo Company (survived until WWII). A useful concrete example of "informal-to-formal" imperial mechanics.

🟥 Common Mistake

Don't treat the Boer War as a straightforward British victory. Plans for British immigration and "Anglicization" never materialized, and Afrikaners effectively won the peace politically. The war's biggest losers were Africans, excluded from the settlement entirely. MacKenzie is explicit: the foundations of apartheid were laid in this period, not later.

🟦 Definition

Extraterritoriality — the right, held by Europeans in Chinese treaty ports, to run their commercial enclaves under their own laws rather than Chinese jurisdiction.

🟦 Definition

Mandates system — the Versailles arrangement under which German and Ottoman territories were placed under victorious powers' administration, nominally supervised by the League of Nations.

⭐ High-Yield Takeaway

1919 marks a genuinely new development: former colonies (South Africa, Australia, New Zealand) became mandate-holding imperial powers in their own right — even as the war that enabled this also began to destabilize the entire postwar imperial order.

🟦 Definition

Balfour Declaration (1926) — proclaimed that Britain and the dominions were "free and equal states, in no way subservient one to the other," formally realized in the 1931 Statute of Westminster.

🟥 Common Mistake

Mussolini's Ethiopian invasion is often treated in isolation. MacKenzie frames it as deliberate revenge for the 1896 Adowa defeat (§1) — "shamelessly opportunistic," entirely unprevented by the western powers, and read by Afro-Asian nationalists as proof of Europe's aggressive decadence.

🟦 Definition

Dyarchy — the mixed system (from the 1935 Act) under which Indians controlled many internal matters while Britain held the central levers of authority, notably defense and foreign policy.

🟥 Common Mistake

Don't treat Partition as a clean administrative solution. A hastily drawn boundary commission left millions stranded on the "wrong" side, triggering the exchange of roughly 10 million Hindus (Pakistan→India) and 7.5 million Muslims (India→Pakistan), with over a million killed. "Pakistan" was a synthetic name from provincial initials; its two-wing structure proved unstable — East Pakistan broke away as Bangladesh (1971).

⭐ High-Yield Takeaway

Hong Kong's 1997 handover was not decolonization to independence but a transfer between empires — British to Chinese. A fitting final note: formal empire ended less by producing free states than by handing authority to a new nation-state order.
