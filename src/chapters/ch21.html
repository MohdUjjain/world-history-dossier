Chapter 21

The United States and the End of Isolation

Study notes: The United States and the End of Isolation, 1939–1941

The United States entered the Second World War through an incremental, strategically calculated retreat from neutrality. Roosevelt connected British survival and resistance to Japan with American security, using changes in law, aid programmes, naval deployments and executive bargains before the attacks of December 1941 made formal participation unavoidable.
1938,1939,1940,1941

Between 1939 and 1941, the United States moved from legislated neutrality toward material support for Britain, naval confrontation with Germany and ultimately world war. Franklin D. Roosevelt regarded an Axis victory as a threat not only to Europe but to American military security, prosperity and political values. The account stresses that the shift was deliberate and incremental—and controversial because executive commitments frequently ran ahead of public assurances.

I Roosevelt’s Interventionist Rationale

Military, economic and ideological fears connected the European war to American security.

Roosevelt’s three arguments against Axis dominance
ThreatRationale
MilitaryAxis control of Europe and Africa might create “jumping-off places” for attacks on the Western Hemisphere; Roosevelt contrasted Africa–Brazil and Washington–Denver distances.
EconomicNazi slave labor could undersell US goods and press American wages and labor standards downward.
IdeologicalRoosevelt portrayed Nazism as hostile to law, liberty, morality and religion; its dominance would threaten democratic life.

These claims transformed aid to Britain from an act of altruism into an argument for American self-preservation. Nevertheless, a vocal isolationist movement continued to insist that oceans, the US Navy and neutrality legislation were sufficient to keep the country out of war.

II The Breakdown of Neutrality, 1938–1940

Washington replaced an arms embargo with carefully limited sales to belligerents.

From the Neutrality Acts to Cash-and-Carry

In the 1930s, Roosevelt accepted laws restricting US loans and arms sales to combatants. Following Kristallnacht (November 1938), he recalled the US ambassador from Berlin. Hitler’s invasion of Poland (September 1939) prompted a push to repeal the arms embargo. In November 1939, cash-and-carry allowed belligerents to buy US munitions if they paid immediately and transported them on their own ships—conditions better suited to sea-power Britain.

France’s Defeat Changed the Calculation

Roosevelt had hoped Britain’s navy could blockade or “quarantine” Germany without a large trans-Channel war. The rapid fall of France in June 1940 destroyed that assumption and intensified the sense of US vulnerability. Congress approved military appropriations totaling around $3.2 billion by mid-1940, exceeding Roosevelt’s initial requests in the historical account.

III A Peacetime Draft and Destroyers for Britain

Emergency preparation brought conscription and far-reaching executive commitments.

The Burke–Wadsworth Act (1940)

The first American peacetime draft aimed to expand the Army toward 500,000. Supporters argued volunteers could not meet the emergency, especially if Axis powers threatened European possessions in the Americas. Opponents warned of militarism and lost civil liberties, asserting that the country was safe behind its large navy.

Destroyers-for-Bases (September 1940)

On 2 September 1940, the United States transferred 50 older destroyers to Britain for 99-year leases on naval and air facilities in the Western Hemisphere, including the Bahamas, Jamaica and Trinidad. Historians Bailey and Ryan emphasize the ships’ strategic value for Atlantic survival; Robert Shogan stresses the dangerous precedent set by a largely secret executive bargain.

IV Lend-Lease: The Crucial Decision

American aid shifted from immediate payment to a direct material stake in British survival.

A Financial Emergency

By late 1940, Britain’s dollar reserves were dwindling. Winston Churchill warned that Britain could not continue without American assistance. Under Lend-Lease, the president could “lease or lend” defence articles to any country whose security was deemed vital to the United States, breaking the cash requirement that had limited earlier aid.

V The Atlantic: An Undeclared Naval War

Roosevelt extended US protection to the sea routes connecting America and Britain.

From Patrol to Confrontation

US forces occupied or secured positions in Greenland and Iceland, essential for Atlantic routes and refuelling. Under a broad Western Hemisphere neutrality patrol, American ships shared information on German submarines with British warships. The patrol boundary reached the 26th meridian.

The Greer Incident and Shoot-on-Sight

After the destroyer Greer exchanged fire with a German U-boat in September 1941, Roosevelt described submarines as the “rattlesnakes of the Atlantic” and announced stronger protections for merchant shipping in vital waters. The US had not formally entered the war, but neutrality was becoming difficult to distinguish from armed support for Britain.

VI The Pacific Deadlock and Entry into War

Economic sanctions narrowed Japan’s choices until the diplomatic process failed.

The Sanctions Escalator

In July 1940, Washington restricted exports of high-octane aviation fuel and scrap iron to Japan. After Japan moved into southern Indochina in July 1941, Roosevelt froze Japanese assets; this effectively halted trade and threatened a navy dependent on imported oil. The account gives Japan roughly 18 months of naval oil reserves.

From Plan A and B to Pearl Harbor

Japanese proposals Plan A and Plan B sought a negotiated restoration of trade and an end to American support for China. Secretary of State Cordell Hull’s ten-point proposal demanded Japanese withdrawal from China and Indochina, which the Japanese military regarded as unacceptable. Japan’s Tripartite Pact (1940) with Germany and Italy meanwhile encouraged Roosevelt to view Europe and the Pacific as one global confrontation.

War Arrives

Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941. Four days later, Hitler declared war on the United States. Roosevelt could now pursue the defeat of Germany—his stated overriding strategic priority—within a formally global war.

§ Conclusion: The End of American Isolation

The road to war involved calculated aid, contested executive power and an escalating Pacific impasse.

Roosevelt presented assistance to Britain as the best way to avoid fighting later and defend American security. Yet legislation, naval deployments and executive agreements drew the US closer to belligerency. The historical assessment remains contested: advocates praise Roosevelt’s reading of the Nazi danger, while critics stress the secrecy, evasions and expansion of presidential authority involved in his gradual approach.
