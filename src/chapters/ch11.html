Chapter 11

Envisioning a New World Order

Study notes: Envisioning a New World Order — The Ideological Battle Between Wilson and Lenin

The Great War destroyed the dynastic order and opened a contest between Woodrow Wilson's liberal internationalism and Vladimir Lenin's revolutionary socialism. Both rejected the imperial system that produced the war, but Wilson imagined peace through democracy, markets and collective security, while Lenin believed capitalism itself must be overthrown by international class revolution.
1917,1918,1919,1920

The collapse of the empires of Nicholas II, Wilhelm II and Franz Joseph transformed Central and Eastern Europe. From that destruction came competing designs for a world beyond the prewar imperial order, advanced by the United States and revolutionary Soviet Russia. The ideological rivalry between Wilson and Lenin has been described as a kind of “First Cold War.”

I Wilson: Liberal Internationalism

Moral force, democracy and open economic exchange against the balance of power.

The Critique of Old Diplomacy

Woodrow Wilson regarded the traditional European balance-of-power system as a self-defeating zero-sum contest. Secret diplomacy, temporary alliances and armaments competition made conflict likely. He believed the United States, drawing on European civilization but claiming no territorial ambition, was unusually placed to defend neutral principles of peace and justice.

Morality, Democracy and the Monroe Doctrine

Wilson grounded foreign policy in Christian moral language, even invoking “holy scripture” and honour. He distinguished the German people from their autocratic rulers and insisted that democratic accountability could underpin reliable international commitments. In the account he also treated the Monroe Doctrine as a model for excluding external intervention from a region without controlling its constituent states' internal affairs.

The Fourteen Points and a League of Nations

In January 1918, Wilson announced his Fourteen Points as the foundation for a just peace. The proposed order combined national self-determination, economic openness, the rights of labour and a voluntary League of Nations to arbitrate disputes. Rather than the “sinister balance of power,” he envisioned an association of nations bound by trust, international opinion and democratic reform. Even initially undemocratic states might eventually choose to join this association of “free and rich nations.”

II Lenin: Capitalism and World Revolution

War is rooted not in bad diplomacy, but in capitalist economic structures.

Vladimir Lenin rejected Wilson's programme as a “utopian dream.” Whether a government was formally democratic or autocratic mattered less than its underlying economic order. As long as the capitalist system survived, the pressure toward imperial conflict would remain.

Imperialism: The Highest Stage of Capitalism

Lenin treated imperialism as an outcome of monopoly capitalism. Free competition gave way to cartels and trusts; bank and industrial interests merged into finance capital; and the search for raw materials, colonies and markets turned economic rivalry into global territorial struggle. The Great War was, in this analysis, a predatory contest for economic gain among capitalist powers.

From Imperialist War to Civil War

Lenin called on the proletariat to stop fighting for the bourgeois state and instead turn the imperialist conflict into class struggle at home: a revolutionary effort to “smash the machinery of the bourgeois state.” The Third International (Comintern), established in 1919, would support communist revolution beyond Russia. The larger objective was an international socialist federation managing the productive forces for the many under a dictatorship of the proletariat.

“The period of imperialism is the eve of the social revolution of the proletariat.” — Vladimir Lenin

III Self-Determination: One Phrase, Two Projects

Political independence and economic emancipation are not the same thing.

For Wilson, the new European states could exercise democratic sovereignty and enter an international community. For Lenin, an “independent” Poland could remain economically subordinate to capitalist powers. Genuine freedom would require changing property and class relations, not simply drawing new frontiers.

IV Popularity and the Limits of Both Visions

Popular hopes run into domestic politics and the failure of world revolution.

Wilson: Acclaim Abroad, Defeat at Home

European crowds in London and Paris welcomed Wilson with extraordinary enthusiasm, seeing hope for a “peace without victory” after years of suffering. Yet the United States Senate would not ratify the Treaty of Versailles. A major Democratic defeat in the 1920 election marked the rejection of his comprehensive internationalist programme at home and deprived his League project of American participation.

Lenin: Revolutionary Appeal, Soviet Isolation

Lenin's appeal reached radical workers and opponents of Allied involvement in the Russian Civil War. “Hands off Russia” campaigns mobilized support, and the American Socialist Party shifted from the Second to the Third International. But anticipated revolutions in Hungary and Bavaria were suppressed, and global socialist upheaval failed to spread. Soviet Russia became an isolated pariah state surrounded by capitalist rivals.

§ Conclusion: An Unfinished New World Order

Neither the liberal league nor global revolution was fully realized.

Wilson and Lenin both recognized the death of the old imperial order and feared the consequences of failing to replace it. Their rival solutions were comprehensive but only partly realized: Wilson's domestic defeat weakened the League, while Lenin's failed worldwide revolution encouraged his successors to turn toward “socialism in one country.” Their clash between liberal internationalism and revolutionary socialism became central to the international history of the twentieth century and left the world vulnerable to further disorder.
