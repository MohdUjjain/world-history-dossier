Chapter 04

A Shrinking World

From Global Networks to Global Consciousness: Speed, Power and Consequences

Jeffrey A. Engel

Global connections long predated the twentieth century; what changed was the unprecedented speed and scale with which people could transmit information, organize markets and cross distances. Engel shows that the same networks strengthened trade and shared culture while also serving empire, warfare, surveillance and environmental change. The shrinking world made new possibilities available, not inevitable peace or political unity.

Engel’s “A Shrinking World” explains how twentieth-century changes in communication and transport allowed information, goods, money, images and people to cross great distances at extraordinary speed. The central idea is , but Engel insists that the process began long before modern inventions. His question is not merely which machines appeared? It is how faster connections changed the opportunities, power and choices of people who used them—sometimes with consequences their inventors never intended.

I An Old Process at Unprecedented Speed

Connectivity has a long history; its twentieth-century reach and speed were new.

Ancient Mediterranean trade and competition, illustrated by the Punic Wars between Rome and Carthage, already depended on control of major routes. Mongol rulers later connected lands from Europe and the Middle East to Asia through roads and communications. Merchants, imperial planners and explorers repeatedly imagined they were creating an integrated world. Engel therefore rejects the claim that the twentieth century invented global connections: it made them more extensive, faster and usable by more people.

In 1904, geographer Halford Mackinder feared that industrial production and railroads could concentrate power in a state controlling Eurasia. Yet later aircraft and wireless signals also made borders more permeable, and people with access to wider information sometimes demanded local autonomy instead of accepting central control. The same technology could help a state coordinate power or help communities challenge it. Its political consequences therefore depended on who controlled and used the network.

Telegraphy, radio, aircraft and eventually the Internet made increasingly possible: people in separate countries could encounter the same news, music, sporting events and political crises. By the century’s end, the fall of communism and Cold War barriers enlarged the market in which people and firms could operate. But access was not universal, and common information did not erase unequal power or local identity. A “shrinking world” describes compressed time and distance, not a disappearance of differences.

Engel also disputes the comforting belief that trade and communication automatically create peace. France and Germany were major trading partners in 1914, yet went to war. Faster exchange could encourage understanding, but could also spread fear, propaganda, threats and military intelligence. This warning governs the chapter: greater interdependence enlarges the possibilities for cooperation and conflict, rather than determining which one people choose.

II Communications: Faster Information, New Forms of Power

The telegraph connected markets; wireless, radio, television and phones transformed security and everyday life.

Telegraphy and the infrastructure of a world market

The nineteenth-century gave states and merchants a way to exchange encoded messages far faster than any ship. Samuel Morse’s code helped standardize signalling. To span oceans engineers needed a well-insulated able to survive pressure, currents and damage. A cable across the Channel failed in 1850 and a stronger link succeeded in 1851; an expensive transatlantic cable worked briefly in 1858 before failing, while a more durable Atlantic connection followed in 1866. Ocean-floor surveys, including work in 1853, helped engineers understand the physical world they needed to cross. Communication projects therefore promoted scientific knowledge as well as commerce.

Imperial planners sought a dependable . By 1865 cable connections linked London with India, but relayed messages could still take days and traverse politically vulnerable territory. British strategists developed a more secure, submerged “All-British” network by the early twentieth century—ironically just as wireless methods were becoming practical. Faster communications were never simply neutral infrastructure: their routes, ownership and security affected imperial and military power.

The market effect came from increasingly timely . Traders could learn likely prices for cotton, grain, textiles or meat while cargo was still at sea; financiers could coordinate sales, shipping, storage and credit with less uncertainty. As a leading hub of the cable network, London consolidated its position in global finance. These links made trade more efficient, but also concentrated influence with traders, banks and states that controlled the information. The telegraph shrank commercial distance by reducing uncertainty before delivery, not by moving goods physically faster.

Telephone access: connection is also a political choice

Alexander Graham Bell patented the telephone in 1876, making speech possible across wires. Over the century, improved cables and satellites extended service and sharply lowered its relative cost: a three-minute US coast-to-coast call cost nearly a working day’s wages in 1925, but less than five minutes’ wages in 1983. Yet Engel contrasts this expansion with communist and authoritarian states that limited private telephone access or supervised calls. Their rulers knew about communications technology; they restricted who could use it. Availability therefore depended on government policy as well as engineering.

Wireless: safety, interception and war

Radio waves made possible without laying continuous cables. Guglielmo Marconi patented his system in 1896, and a transatlantic radio signal followed in 1901. Insurers encouraged ship-to-shore sets; emergency wireless calls helped rescuers reach survivors of the Titanic. But signals travelling through open air could also be intercepted, spurring and military codebreaking. In 1917, the intercepted German Zimmermann Telegram to Mexico intensified US hostility to Germany. In the Second World War, the interception and decoding of Axis messages, including traffic protected by Enigma, showed that secure communications could decide military advantages. Faster messages aided rescue and coordination but could also accelerate crisis and expose strategic secrets.

Radio and television: from private signals to shared publics

Once allowed one station to reach many listeners, radio helped establish news and entertainment as daily habits. Governments assigned frequencies, as in the US Radio Act of 1912, while advertising and commercial broadcasters sought audiences and revenue. Mass broadcasting could distribute information and solidarity, but also strengthen propaganda. Military, political and business interests all competed to control the airwaves.

Television extended radio’s shared experience to moving images. The term was used in 1900; John Logie Baird demonstrated wireless image transmission in 1925, and the Nazi government used television rooms to show the 1936 Berlin Olympics. The Depression and war delayed wider commercial growth, but after 1945 television ownership surged. Engel reports that US homes with sets rose from about 8,000 in 1946 to more than 45 million in 1960. Communications satellites in the 1960s, then cable and satellite systems, broadened reach and specialized programming.

Images of the 1969 Moon landing, the 1989 Berlin Wall celebrations and the crackdown at Tiananmen Square helped make political events visible far from where they occurred. International sport and entertainment created recognisable figures such as Michael Jordan and David Beckham. These networks promoted through widely shared products and images, while also provoking criticism of American cultural dominance. Greater worldwide visibility produced common reference points and reactions against cultural influence.

Communications reshaped diplomacy too: following the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis, Washington and Moscow established a direct hotline to reduce the risks of delay and misunderstanding. Satellite telephony, mobile communications and the Internet then multiplied ways to speak across borders. Yet global messaging could be used by governments, protesters, firms and violent organizations alike. By century’s end the world was more immediately connected, not necessarily more peaceful or politically unified.

III Travel: From Mass Automobility to Global Flight

Physical movement became faster and cheaper, while restructuring landscapes, economies and security.

The automobile: invention becomes a mass social system

The automobile depended on the , developed in the nineteenth century through work by Étienne Lenoir and Nikolaus Otto, among others. Karl Benz and Gottlieb Daimler helped advance early vehicles. Yet useful engines did not by themselves create a mass market: cars were expensive, handmade and difficult to produce in large numbers. Henry Ford neither invented the car nor originated the assembly line. Instead, he perfected with standardized parts and specialized tasks. The 1908 Model T, initially priced at $825 in Engel’s account, reached a wider market. A production-time example Engel gives for 1913 falls from about 12 hours 30 minutes to under three hours, showing how organization could transform access to an invention.

Widespread reshaped where people worked, lived and shopped. Roads, suburbs, trucking networks and highways were built around vehicles; the 1956 US Interstate Highway Act represented this development on a national scale, with civilian and Cold War strategic motives. Ownership became a mark of prosperity: Engel cites French workers’ car ownership rising from roughly 8% in 1953 to about half fourteen years later. The car was no longer a machine for a few wealthy people—it organized cities, commerce and daily routines.

Growth brought serious costs. Cheap gasoline encouraged larger US cars after 1945, but the 1970s oil crises and growing European and Japanese competition increased interest in smaller and more efficient vehicles. This revealed : oil prices and access became economic and national-security concerns. Automobile exhaust contributed to smog and air pollution, though Engel also identifies industry and energy production as causes of deadly episodes such as London’s 1952 smog. Ralph Nader’s Unsafe at Any Speed (1965) helped provoke safety reforms, including US laws passed in 1966. Mass car ownership expanded individual mobility while creating shared environmental, safety and strategic burdens.

Aviation: military demand precedes ordinary travel

The Wright brothers’ controlled powered flight of 1903 lasted 59 seconds. Louis Blériot’s crossing of the English Channel in 1909 showed that seas no longer guaranteed security. The Wrights themselves sought military customers, and the First World War rapidly increased the production and destructive capacity of aircraft. Bombers made civilian cities vulnerable; aircraft also became more capable of crossing long distances. A machine promising travel and contact first gained massive investment because states wanted military advantage.

After the war, grew through mail subsidies, government contracts and a demand for faster travel. Juan Trippe’s Pan American used mail and passenger routes to connect the Americas, then expanded across the Pacific. These routes helped project US business and influence, not merely transport holidaymakers. Airships such as the Graf Zeppelin briefly competed for long-distance traffic, but the Hindenburg disaster in 1937 weakened confidence in them. Passenger planes like the Douglas DC-3 made regular air travel safer, more economical and more attractive.

Wartime engine research later supported in civilian passenger service. Britain’s de Havilland Comet entered service in 1952, offering fast, high-altitude travel, but fatal accidents led to the fleet’s grounding in 1954. American firms, supported by military orders and shared designs between civil and military aircraft, gained a commanding commercial position; Airbus later challenged this dominance. The Concorde illustrated the opposite commercial lesson: exceptional speed did not ensure profitability. With large airliners such as the Boeing 747 and Airbus A380, what had required weeks by sea could increasingly be achieved within a day by air. Aviation brought people nearer while remaining shaped by security, government subsidies and competition.

IV The Shrinking World’s Unfinished Consequences

Engel’s conclusion joins technological opportunity with political choice and unintended costs.

Engel treats communications and transport as forms of . The same airplane could carry passengers or bombs; radio could summon rescue or transmit orders; television could educate people or spread narrow messages; networked communication could help reformers organize or help criminal groups. These outcomes are not inherent moral instructions within the machine. Who owns it, controls access and chooses its purpose shapes how it changes society.

The wider consequences reach beyond inventions. Networks could make markets more efficient and firms more global, but concentrate financial power; expand shared cultural experiences, but provoke resistance to cultural dominance; support international understanding, but also accelerate diplomatic crises; spread private mobility, but increase oil dependence, danger and pollution. Engel’s examples therefore resist a simple narrative that technology brings either inevitable liberation or inevitable control.

The conclusion returns to the opening question. Earlier societies also built long-distance connections and imagined they lived in a new global age. The twentieth century was distinctive for the unprecedented speed, scale and simultaneous use of its networks. It shrank the practical distance between people without settling how they would coexist. Globalization remained a continuing process, not a completed peaceful destination—and its future depended on the human choices made through these technologies.

DEFINITION
Codebreaking

Recovering the meaning of intercepted encoded messages, often without permission or knowledge of the sender. Contrast: intercepting a radio signal is not the same task as deciphering its contents.

HIGH-YIELD FINDING
Apply the telegraph to a trading decision

A shipment of grain is still at sea. A cable reports a changed market price. What decisions can a merchant adjust before arrival? Think: buyers, storage, loans and onward shipping. That is the mechanism behind greater efficiency.

EXAM INSIGHT
Compare two wartime uses

Titanic: a signal alerts rescuers. Zimmermann: an intercepted message intensifies diplomatic hostility. For an essay, show how access, security and purpose—not speed alone—explain the outcomes.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION
Do not equate invention with access

Engel contrasts cheap and widespread calling in some countries with restricted, supervised telephone access elsewhere. When explaining diffusion, ask who was allowed to connect and at what cost.
