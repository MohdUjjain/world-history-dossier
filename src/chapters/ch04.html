Chapter 04

A Shrinking World

Study notes: The 20th Century’s Acceleration of Globalism

Global interconnectedness is an ancient process. What made the twentieth century distinctive was not the invention of globalism, but its unprecedented acceleration: transport and communication compressed distance, time and the boundaries of political, economic and cultural life.
1853,1876,1890,1903,1913,1946,1952,1960,1969,1989

Jeffrey A. Engel’s account of a shrinking world traces the technological, economic and social transformation of previously distant regions into an increasingly connected global marketplace and shared consciousness. It is an argument about the pace of change, not simply a succession of inventions.

I A Process of Pace, Not Just Innovation

The desire to bridge distance is ancient; twentieth-century speed was new.

Long before the twentieth century, people crossed frontiers for trade, power and movement: the Punic Wars and the road networks of Genghis Khan show that linking distant places was never a modern invention. Earlier generations also regarded themselves as pioneers of interconnection.

What changed was the ability to sustain truly global political, financial and cultural structures. Real-time electronic communication and journeys completed in hours compressed time and space. Following the fall of communism and the end of the Cold War, the century saw the emergence of a practically universal marketplace.

II The Communications Revolution

Information moved from physical wires to invisible signals and shared images.

Telegraphy: The First High-Speed Information Network

The electric telegraph was already central to international relations at the century’s start. Morse code transmitted information across long distances by wire, making rapid exchange possible before goods or people could arrive. The search for transoceanic cables led to the first scientific mapping of the ocean floor in 1853—an example of commercial demand stimulating scientific investigation.

Economic centralization: Traders could learn world prices long before shipments arrived. By linking markets in American cotton, Indian rice and Australian beef, telegraphy helped turn London into the global banking hub. Faster information reshaped financial authority as well as commerce.

Telephony: A Voice on the Wire

Alexander Graham Bell patented the telephone in 1876. Unlike the telegraph, it brought human speech into electronic communications. The expense of a long-distance call then fell dramatically over the century.

Wireless, Radio and the Paradox of Peace

In the 1890s, Guglielmo Marconi helped pioneer radio and wireless communication, freeing messages from the need for continuous physical cables. Wireless became crucial for maritime safety, notably during the Titanic disaster, and for military strategy.

Promoters repeatedly claimed that easier communication would foster peace. Yet the Zimmerman Telegram (1917) demonstrated another possibility: rapid but insecure information could inflame passions and accelerate conflict. Connectivity was not automatically a force for harmony.

Television and the Making of Global Consciousness

Envisioned at the 1900 World’s Fair and developed through the cathode-ray tube, television became a dominant cultural medium after 1945. US household television ownership grew from roughly 8,000 in 1946 to more than 45 million by 1960.

Television enabled simultaneous or shared viewing of events such as the 1969 Moon landing, the 1989 fall of the Berlin Wall, and the Tiananmen Square crackdown. It also produced cross-border celebrities—from Michael Jordan to David Beckham—whose public recognition could exceed that of political or religious leaders.

III The Travel Revolution

Internal combustion and aerodynamics transformed physical movement.

The Automobile: Mass Production and Its Costs

Cars moved from expensive curiosities for enthusiasts to a central feature of mass consumption. Henry Ford did not invent the automobile or the assembly line; he perfected the assembly-line method for automobile production. In 1913, production time fell from about 12.5 hours to under three hours per car.

Automobility changed landscapes and cities, prompting government spending on large transport networks, including the 1956 Interstate Highway Act in the United States. By the 1960s its darker consequences had become apparent: Los Angeles smog, traffic hazards and safety criticisms such as Ralph Nader’s Unsafe at Any Speed.

Aviation: Kitty Hawk to the Jet Age

Air travel was overwhelmingly a twentieth-century transformation. The Wright brothers’ first flight in 1903 lasted 59 seconds. Military contracts accelerated aviation; observers already understood by 1909 that even the English Channel could no longer guarantee geographical isolation.

The Douglas DC-3 made profitable transcontinental commercial travel increasingly practical during the 1930s. The British de Havilland Comet inaugurated commercial jet service in 1952, but serious technical failures and crashes eventually helped American manufacturers, including Boeing, take the lead.

“The A-380, and the Boeing 747 before it, each offers a wingspan longer than the total distance of the Wright brothers’ first flight.”

Conclusion: The Neutrality of Technology

Technology is morally neutral. An airplane can reconnect families or deliver bombs; television can inform viewers or encourage sedentary living; the Internet can organize social change or facilitate criminal networks. The decisive factor is the human will to use the tools.

The century’s overarching result was the annihilation of time and distance. Connectivity was an ancient aspiration, but its new speed affected nearly every part of political, financial and cultural life. Globalization was therefore not a finished destination but an accelerating process continuing into the twenty-first century.
