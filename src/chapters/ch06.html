Chapter 06

The Triple Alliance

Study notes: The Irony of Stability — The Rise and Fall of the Triple Alliance

Jürgen Angelow and Gordon Martel

The Triple Alliance was intended to protect newly established political orders, restrain allies, and avert war. For decades, the European alliance system helped encourage peace. But by 1914 Germany and Austria-Hungary increasingly believed security required taking the risk of war, while Italy doubted that joining them would protect its interests. Why did the two linked defensive alliances develop in such different directions?
1871,1878,1879,1881,1882,1887,1890,1902,1914,1915

Angelow and Martel ask how an alliance among three countries with different interests could preserve peace for decades and then fail. Germany sought security for the Reich created in 1871; Austria-Hungary feared Russian pressure and nationalist challenges; Italy wanted protection against France and greater influence in the Mediterranean. The problem was not merely the existence of treaties but the changing purposes and expectations of the governments that signed them.

I The Genesis of a New Order, 1871–1879

New and reconstructed powers fear being undone by war.

Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy had all been made or remade through war between 1859 and 1871. Their governments feared that another war could destroy the states and constitutional settlements recently created by victory, defeat and compromise. They therefore shared an interest in stability, even while retaining ambitions and rivalries of their own.

The Congress of Berlin, 1878

After the Russo-Turkish War, Bismarck presented himself at the Congress of Berlin as an “honest broker.” By cutting down the created under Russian auspices, the Congress avoided an immediate confrontation but disappointed St. Petersburg. Russia believed its battlefield achievements had been taken away and blamed Germany's chancellor, deepening Russo-German friction.

Bismarck feared a coalition of : a France determined to undo its defeat joined by a Russia hostile to Berlin. His strategic answer was to remain —one of three in a Europe of five Great Powers—so Germany would never face a hostile combination alone.

The three future alliance members were transformed by war: Prussia built the Reich through victories from 1864 to 1871; Austria-Hungary emerged as the Dual Monarchy after the Habsburg defeat of 1866; Italy unified with French military and British diplomatic help but still disputed Austrian-held territories. States made or remade through force feared that new wars could destroy them. Yet the post-1871 also discouraged a general war until 1914, because conflict between two powers could pull many others into fighting.

The 1878 Congress of Berlin revised Russia’s gains after the Russo-Turkish War, especially the proposed much larger Bulgaria. Russia sought Balkan influence and access to the Black Sea Straits, but Austria-Hungary and Britain feared such expansion. The settlement averted immediate war while deepening Russian mistrust of Germany. Bismarck feared that Russia might then join a France seeking revenge for Alsace-Lorraine. He tried to keep both Vienna and St Petersburg connected to Berlin and encouraged French colonial ambitions in North Africa to divert attention from the Rhine.

II Constructing the Bismarckian System

Overlapping commitments designed to reassure, deter and restrain.

The Dual Alliance, 1879

Germany and Austria-Hungary concluded a . If either were attacked by Russia, the other would provide assistance; if attacked by another power, notably France, the partner would maintain . Its principal purpose was deterrence: Russia would hesitate to challenge Vienna in the Balkans if Berlin might also become an opponent.

The Three Emperors’ League and Reinsurance Treaty

Bismarck also tried to keep Russia within his diplomatic system. The of 1881 emphasized monarchical solidarity against republicanism and radical social movements. Austro-Russian disputes over Bulgaria undermined it. Germany then signed the with Russia in 1887, a secret bilateral undertaking of neutrality.

The Triple Alliance, 1882: The Triplice

Italy joined Germany and Austria-Hungary in 1882, despite Italy's historic antagonism with Vienna. The impetus included the : France's 1881 annexation of Tunis frustrated Italian ambitions and helped push Rome toward Berlin. For Germany, the pact was an instrument of “surveillance and pacification”, intended to contain its allies' rival interests as much as to defend them.

These treaties were different, not four versions of one alliance. The 1879 Dual Alliance required aid if Russia attacked either party, but usually benevolent neutrality in a war against another power; obligations strengthened if Russia supported the attacker. The 1881 Three Emperors’ League promoted consultation and conservative solidarity with Russia, but disputes over Bulgaria destroyed it. Bismarck therefore signed the secret 1887 Reinsurance Treaty to prevent Russian hostility from becoming permanent. The 1882 Triplice was mainly defensive and did not automatically require support for an ally’s offensive war.

Italy entered the Triplice despite old disputes with Austria over Tyrol and Trieste. The alliance was a bargain among monarchs and their cabinets, not a popular national movement; Rome was treated as the less powerful member. Italy emphasized through the Mancini Declaration that the alliance was not aimed at Britain, whose Mediterranean fleet threatened its long coastline. During of 1885–87—Austro-Russian disputes over Bulgaria and renewed Franco-German tensions—Bismarck drew Britain toward Italy and Austria through Mediterranean understandings, but Britain never joined the Triple Alliance. Italy secured greater North African recognition and Balkan compensation provisions in the 1887 renewal without ending the rivalry with Vienna.

III The “New Course” and the Erosion of Stability

German global ambitions loosen the alliance's internal bonds.

After Bismarck's resignation in 1890, German policy increasingly turned away from his continental emphasis. Under a “New Course” associated with Bernhard von Bülow, Germany pursued —a world policy marked by global ambition—and began constructing a high-seas fleet in 1897. This pursuit of imperial standing aggravated relations with Britain and unsettled the alliance network.

Contradictions within the Alliance

Italy's nationalists continued to demand , including the Austrian-held Tyrol and Trieste. Austria-Hungary and Russia competed in the Balkans; Italy and France contested interests in North Africa; Germany and France remained antagonists around the Rhine. These intersecting pressures made the alliance an uneasy instrument of restraint rather than a shared expansionist project.

Allies Seek Security Elsewhere

As Berlin's policy became less predictable, its partners made independent diplomatic arrangements. Austria-Hungary and Russia agreed to supervise Balkan affairs in the of 1903 and made a neutrality agreement in 1904. Italy and France repaired their relationship; in 1902, Italy promised neutrality if France became involved in an unprovoked war. These agreements gave members alternative partners and weakened their dependence on the Triple Alliance.

The Franco-Russian alignment of 1891–93 could at first encourage caution by strengthening the balance of power. Italy renewed its agreement in 1891 while pursuing colonial ambitions under Francesco Crispi; its defeat at Adowa demonstrated that alliance membership did not ensure colonial success. Germany’s new naval policy from 1897 worsened relations with Britain, unsettling Vienna and Rome. Austria-Hungary instead negotiated with Russia through the Mürzsteg Convention of 1903 and the neutrality pact of 1904. Italy promised France neutrality in some circumstances in 1902 without formally leaving the Triplice.

The contradictions became visible in crises. Italy followed its French understanding during the Moroccan dispute of 1905. Germany backed Austria-Hungary’s 1908 annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina, whereas Italy sought a conference. Rome then pursued Libya during the 1911–12 war with the Ottoman Empire without much allied help. The Balkan Wars of 1912–13 made Italy and Austria-Hungary clash over Albania, and Vienna’s military planners even considered preventive war against Italy. By the eve of 1914, formal treaties remained intact while the partners increasingly looked elsewhere for security.

IV Military Failure and the July Crisis

Defensive treaties fail to create a common military strategy.

Uncoordinated War Plans

Political obligations were not matched by meaningful operational integration. After 1897, Germany and Austria-Hungary lacked significant coordinated military arrangements. German planning assumed an attack on France in the west while Austria contained Russia in the east. Austrian planning instead prioritized a campaign against Serbia, assuming Germany would shield it from Russia. Both relied on expectations the other had not accepted.

Italy's commitment was doubted in Berlin and Vienna. By 1912, the Italian position suggested that mobilization did not guarantee troops would actually deploy in Germany's support. The members had a formal alliance but no dependable common plan for a continental war.

July 1914: Consultation Breaks Down

After the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, Vienna treated the crisis as an opportunity to resolve the perceived Serbian threat. Berlin backed this approach, fearing that the balance of power was shifting toward the Franco-Russian grouping and the wider Triple Entente. Germany and Austria-Hungary did not consult Italy as their arrangements required, partly because they distrusted its loyalties.

Italy used that absence of consultation, together with the pact's defensive character, to justify neutrality in August 1914. In 1915, Italy entered the war on the Entente side following promises of more attractive territorial gains. The alliance built to secure the Central Powers had failed at the moment it was most needed.

The authors do not argue that alliances inevitably caused war from their creation. They initially helped preserve peace and restrain territorial disputes, but they did not remove the rival ambitions of their members. By 1914 the Dual Alliance kept Germany and Austria-Hungary together even as their governments accepted the risks of war, while the Triple Alliance could not convince Italy to join them. Security arrangements designed to protect monarchies thus contributed to the conflict that destroyed the Hohenzollern and Habsburg orders.

The military arrangements were even less dependable than the diplomatic promises. Germany planned to concentrate its forces against France and expected Austria-Hungary to face Russia; Vienna planned to attack Serbia while counting on German protection from Russia. Their staff talks had not reconciled those priorities. Italian undertakings from 1888, naval discussions in 1900 and the 1913 naval convention also failed to create a reliable common operational plan; by 1912 Italy had spoken of mobilizing without necessarily deploying its forces.

After Franz Ferdinand’s assassination, Austria-Hungary treated a war against Serbia as necessary to protect its status, while German leaders feared that delaying would strengthen the Franco-Russian combination. Berlin and Vienna accepted the risk of a general war and did not properly consult Italy. Rome treated the offensive nature of the Serbian war and lack of consultation as grounds for neutrality. The authors emphasize that Italy was making a political choice, not merely obeying an automatic treaty mechanism.

Italy subsequently bargained with both sides for compensation. It terminated its Triple Alliance commitment on 3 May 1915 and declared war on Austria-Hungary on 23 May 1915. Angelow and Martel do not say Italy’s entry alone determined the final outcome, but its neutrality in 1914 deprived Berlin and Vienna of resources they considered essential. The Dual Alliance remained effective by supporting an aggressive interpretation of defense; the Triple Alliance failed because Rome no longer believed participation served its interests.

“The Triple Alliance was not a combination of ‘nations’ but one of monarchs and their cabinets ... intended to work against the republican and anticlerical movements ... to assure the maintenance of social and political order.”

The alliance designed to safeguard the monarchical principle and social order became part of the process that destroyed both. The “Darwinian” European world Bismarck had sought to manage ultimately consumed his political system.
