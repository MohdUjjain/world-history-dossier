Chapter 06

The Triple Alliance

Study notes: The Irony of Stability — The Rise and Fall of the Triple Alliance

Jürgen Angelow and Gordon Martel

The Triple Alliance was intended to protect newly established political orders, restrain allies, and avert war. For decades, the European alliance system helped encourage peace. But by 1914 Germany and Austria-Hungary increasingly believed security required taking the risk of war, while Italy doubted that joining them would protect its interests. Why did the two linked defensive alliances develop in such different directions?
1871,1878,1879,1881,1882,1887,1890,1902,1914,1915

Angelow and Martel ask how an alliance among three countries with different interests could preserve peace for decades and then fail. Germany sought security for the Reich created in 1871; Austria-Hungary feared Russian pressure and nationalist challenges; Italy wanted protection against France and greater influence in the Mediterranean. The problem was not merely the existence of treaties but the changing purposes and expectations of the governments that signed them.

I The Genesis of a New Order, 1871–1879

New and reconstructed powers fear being undone by war.

Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy had all been made or remade through war between 1859 and 1871. Their governments feared that another war could destroy the states and constitutional settlements recently created by victory, defeat and compromise. They therefore shared an interest in stability, even while retaining ambitions and rivalries of their own.

The Congress of Berlin, 1878

After the Russo-Turkish War, Bismarck presented himself at the Congress of Berlin as an “honest broker.” By cutting down the created under Russian auspices, the Congress avoided an immediate confrontation but disappointed St. Petersburg. Russia believed its battlefield achievements had been taken away and blamed Germany's chancellor, deepening Russo-German friction.

Bismarck feared a coalition of : a France determined to undo its defeat joined by a Russia hostile to Berlin. His strategic answer was to remain —one of three in a Europe of five Great Powers—so Germany would never face a hostile combination alone.

The three future alliance members were transformed by war: Prussia built the Reich through victories from 1864 to 1871; Austria-Hungary emerged as the Dual Monarchy after the Habsburg defeat of 1866; Italy unified with French military and British diplomatic help but still disputed Austrian-held territories. States made or remade through force feared that new wars could destroy them. Yet the post-1871 also discouraged a general war until 1914, because conflict between two powers could pull many others into fighting.

The 1878 Congress of Berlin revised Russia’s gains after the Russo-Turkish War, especially the proposed much larger Bulgaria. Russia sought Balkan influence and access to the Black Sea Straits, but Austria-Hungary and Britain feared such expansion. The settlement averted immediate war while deepening Russian mistrust of Germany. Bismarck feared that Russia might then join a France seeking revenge for Alsace-Lorraine. He tried to keep both Vienna and St Petersburg connected to Berlin and encouraged French colonial ambitions in North Africa to divert attention from the Rhine.

II Constructing the Bismarckian System

Overlapping commitments designed to reassure, deter and restrain.

The Dual Alliance, 1879

Germany and Austria-Hungary concluded a . If either were attacked by Russia, the other would provide assistance; if attacked by another power, notably France, the partner would maintain . Its principal purpose was deterrence: Russia would hesitate to challenge Vienna in the Balkans if Berlin might also become an opponent.

The Three Emperors’ League and Reinsurance Treaty

Bismarck also tried to keep Russia within his diplomatic system. The of 1881 emphasized monarchical solidarity against republicanism and radical social movements. Austro-Russian disputes over Bulgaria undermined it. Germany then signed the with Russia in 1887, a secret bilateral undertaking of neutrality.

The Triple Alliance, 1882: The Triplice

Italy joined Germany and Austria-Hungary in 1882, despite Italy's historic antagonism with Vienna. The impetus included the : France's 1881 annexation of Tunis frustrated Italian ambitions and helped push Rome toward Berlin. For Germany, the pact was an instrument of “surveillance and pacification”, intended to contain its allies' rival interests as much as to defend them.

These agreements were not interchangeable. A military guarantee required assistance only under defined circumstances; a league of monarchs promoted consultation without eliminating their rival interests; a secret neutrality treaty could preserve German–Russian relations yet appear disloyal to Vienna if revealed. Bismarck combined them to reduce the chance of war, not to create one unified command. That distinction explains why the formal alliance system could be extensive while its members remained uncertain of one another’s behavior.

Italy entered the Triplice despite old disputes with Austria over Tyrol and Trieste. The alliance was a bargain among monarchs and their cabinets, not a popular national movement; Rome was treated as the less powerful member. Italy emphasized through the Mancini Declaration that the alliance was not aimed at Britain, whose Mediterranean fleet threatened its long coastline. During of 1885–87—Austro-Russian disputes over Bulgaria and renewed Franco-German tensions—Bismarck drew Britain toward Italy and Austria through Mediterranean understandings, but Britain never joined the Triple Alliance. Italy secured greater North African recognition and Balkan compensation provisions in the 1887 renewal without ending the rivalry with Vienna.

III The “New Course” and the Erosion of Stability

German global ambitions loosen the alliance's internal bonds.

1. Countervailing alliances and Germany’s new ambitions

Bismarck resigned in 1890, and his successors did not renew the Reinsurance Treaty with Russia. France and Russia developed their own countervailing alignment, marked by the French fleet’s visit to Kronstadt in 1891 and a military convention in 1893. For a time the resulting balance could encourage restraint: neither coalition wished to risk a general war. Meanwhile, Italy renewed the Triple Alliance in 1891 but continued colonial expansion under Francesco Crispi. Its defeat at Adowa damaged Italy’s imperial standing and demonstrated that treaty membership did not guarantee success overseas.

Germany then moved toward , a world-power policy associated with Bernhard von Bülow and a high-seas fleet begun in 1897. Berlin hoped Britain would eventually need German support against French and Russian imperial competition. Britain instead resisted entry into the alliance, while naval rivalry intensified. Vienna and Rome had no wish to inherit a conflict with Britain that their alliance had not originally contemplated. German global ambitions therefore made its existing allies less confident in German leadership.

2. Allies found security outside the Triple Alliance

Italy still pursued , including claims to Austrian-held Tyrol and Trieste, while also seeking cooperation with France over North Africa. The improvement of Franco-Italian relations culminated in a 1902 neutrality understanding: Rome promised not to fight France in certain wars France had not provoked. Italy remained a formal member of the Triple Alliance, but France could no longer assume it would be Germany’s automatic enemy. This distinction between treaty membership and practical diplomatic choice became increasingly important.

Austria-Hungary similarly sought to reduce its dependence on Berlin. It improved relations with Russia from 1897, reached the of 1903 for joint supervision of Balkan affairs, and agreed to benevolent neutrality with Russia in 1904. Such agreements reduced immediate Austro-Russian tension but could leave Germany unable to rely on Austria-Hungary in a conflict with Russia. Far from ending the Triple Alliance at once, these separate understandings made its obligations harder to predict.

3. Successive crises reveal the divisions, 1905–1913

During the Moroccan crisis of 1905, Germany expected Italian diplomatic support, but Rome followed its French understanding at Algeciras. The Austro-Russian settlement weakened after Russia’s war with Japan and renewed focus on the Balkans. In 1908 Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia-Herzegovina, which it had administered since 1878 but which had remained formally Ottoman territory. Berlin supported Vienna’s initiative, Russia was forced to accept it, and Italy preferred a conference. The Dual Alliance was moving away from simply defending territory toward supporting Austrian attempts to change the Balkan order.

In the 1911 Moroccan crisis, Italy seized an opportunity to pursue Libya during its war against the Ottoman Empire, without the sustained support it had expected from its partners. The Balkan Wars of 1912–13 sharpened Austro-Italian rivalry over Albania. Austrian military planners even considered a preventive war against Italy, though they did not carry it out. Controversy over a German military mission to Constantinople in 1913 added to the tension. The alliance persisted legally, but each government increasingly pursued its own interests even when they conflicted with a partner’s. This was the erosion of solidarity that would matter in 1914.

IV Military Failure and the July Crisis

Defensive treaties fail to create a common military strategy.

1. Military planning had not caught up with diplomacy

Berlin and Vienna made military contacts after the Dual Alliance, but their plans diverged as Germany concentrated more forces on an offensive against France. Berlin assumed Austria-Hungary would contain Russia in the east. Vienna, however, saw Serbia as its more urgent opponent and assumed Germany would cover the Russian danger. Even renewed staff discussions after 1909 did not solve that contradiction. Each partner counted on military help the other had not adequately planned to provide.

Italian coordination was weaker still. An arrangement of 1888 envisaged Italian troops supporting Germany against France, while a naval agreement in 1900 merely divided operating zones rather than integrating fleets. A three-power Mediterranean naval convention followed in 1913, but little confidence resulted. In 1912 Italy had been willing to promise mobilization without clearly promising deployment; even later assurances of Rhine-bound forces did not convince Berlin and Vienna that Italy would actually fight. The alliances were political understandings without a dependable common operational plan.

2. The July Crisis widened the meaning of defense

After the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in June 1914, Austria-Hungary decided to confront Serbia in order to protect the Habsburg monarchy from nationalist challenges. German leaders feared that the Franco-Russian coalition was growing stronger and that failure to assist Vienna might isolate Germany. Both governments judged a risky war now preferable to what they perceived as a weaker position later. This offensive initiative justified as necessary for security differed sharply from Bismarck’s original efforts to restrain allies from creating crises.

Germany and Austria-Hungary did not properly consult Italy about the Serbian policy, partly because they already doubted Rome’s loyalty. Rome regarded the conflict as outside the alliance’s defensive conditions and treated the lack of consultation as a further reason not to participate. Italian neutrality in August 1914 was therefore not automatic: the authors emphasize that the Italian government could have interpreted its obligations and interests differently. But Berlin and Vienna’s distrust helped produce the exclusion that then strengthened Italy’s decision to remain neutral.

3. From neutrality to the end of the Triplice, 1915

Once war began, Rome negotiated with both camps for compensation. Austria-Hungary initially refused major concessions and later offered too little, even under German pressure. The Entente’s inducements were more attractive. Italy formally terminated its Triple Alliance commitment on 3 May 1915 and declared war on Austria-Hungary on 23 May 1915. Angelow and Martel do not claim that Italian entry alone determined the final outcome. They do stress that Italian neutrality in 1914 redirected resources Berlin and Vienna considered essential for a rapid victory.

The chapter closes with a distinction rather than a simple verdict. For decades, the alliances had helped preserve peace by restraining aggressive moves and increasing the costs of a general war. But they did not resolve clashes over France, Italy or the Balkans. In 1914 the Dual Alliance tightened because Germany and Austria-Hungary believed they could survive only by standing together, even at the risk of war. The Triple Alliance fractured because Italy no longer saw loyalty as securing its interests. Thus a defensive system designed to preserve European monarchies contributed to the war that helped destroy the Hohenzollern and Habsburg political orders.
