Chapter 30

War and Peace in the Global Community, 1989–2001

Study notes: Wilsonianism, Globalization and the Fragile Global Community

1989–2001 appeared to offer a third chance to construct a Wilsonian order around self-determination, open markets and collective security. Yet selective intervention, ethnic conflict and unequal globalization revealed the limits of American leadership and the tension between integration and fragmentation.
1918,1945,1989,1990,1991,1993,1994,1995,1999,2001

The collapse of Soviet power encouraged a renewed belief that democracy, capitalism and collective security could define a global community. After failed or frustrated opportunities in 1919 and 1945, the post-1989 decade seemed to offer a “third chance” for liberal internationalism. The contrast between this optimism and the conflicts of the 1990s is the chapter’s organizing problem.

I Wilsonianism Reborn: The New Order’s Ideals

The triad offered an attractive vocabulary for a post-Cold War settlement.

The Wilsonian triad combined national self-determination, an “Open Door” political economy, and collective security. Its supporters proposed replacing power rivalry with democratic government, unhindered commercial exchange and international action against aggression. The downfall of the Soviet empire suggested that the alternative ideological model had disappeared.

An “End of History”?

Francis Fukuyama interpreted the victory of liberal democracy as leaving no viable rival system, while Michael Mandelbaum called the triad “the ideas that conquered the world.” These were claims about the era’s prevailing worldview, not evidence that political conflict itself had ended.

II Bush and the “New World Order”, 1989–1991

Collective security achieved a conspicuous success, but American power retained limits.

Kuwait as the Test Case

After Iraq invaded Kuwait in 1990, President George H. W. Bush assembled a multinational response authorized through the UN Security Council. Operations Desert Shield and Desert Storm were framed as defending international law: a world governed by the “rule of law, not the rule of the jungle.” With the Cold War veto rivalry diminished, the UN could act more collectively.

Restraint After Victory

Bush’s interpretation of the New World Order remained pragmatic. The coalition liberated Kuwait but did not make regime change in Baghdad or democratizing the Middle East its objective. The outcome demonstrated that collective security could defend a border without transforming the politics of the aggressor state.

The Limits Revealed by China

Following the 1989 Tiananmen Square crackdown, the Bush administration had little means or willingness to impose political change within China. Strategic stability and diplomatic relations competed with democratic ideals; this revealed the uneven reach of universalist rhetoric.

The Friedman Framework

Thomas L. Friedman described an interaction among three actors: states, global capital markets or “supermarkets”, and “super-empowered individuals.” Investors formed an “Electronic Herd” that could rapidly shift funds across borders. Governments seeking international capital were pressured to don a “Golden Straitjacket” of market-friendly rules.

The New Vulnerability

For proponents, competition and investment spread prosperity and the “fantasy of America.” For critics, a system exposed to capital flight made elected governments vulnerable to external market discipline, and the gains of integration were not evenly shared.

IV Clinton: Integration and the Politics of Identity

A more connected world could simultaneously become a more divided one.

New Rules for Trade

President Bill Clinton treated globalization as an “inexorable logic.” The United States backed NAFTA (1994) and the World Trade Organization (1995) as institutions of an expanding open-market order.

“Jihad vs. McWorld”

Benjamin Barber described opposing pressures: McWorld, a consumer-centered global market culture that privileged profit over citizenship; and “Jihad,” used in his argument as shorthand for exclusionary identity politics, communal antagonism and demands for belonging. These are analytical metaphors, not interchangeable descriptions of religious communities.

V The Balkans and NATO after the Cold War

Self-determination and collective security collided in Europe itself.

Yugoslavia’s Dissolution

The breakup of Yugoslavia exposed how nationalism could produce state fragmentation and ethnic cleansing, rather than orderly independence. Slobodan Milošević’s ambitions and conflicts over territory challenged the Wilsonian belief that national self-government necessarily produced peace.

Bosnia and Kosovo

US-led pressure and NATO air operations helped lead to the Dayton Peace Accords (1995), which preserved Bosnia-Herzegovina within a complex settlement. NATO later intervened militarily over Kosovo (1999), invoking protection of Kosovar Albanians; its legal and political implications remained contested.

NATO’s Change of Purpose

The alliance moved beyond its original anti-Soviet role, admitted Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic, and developed cooperation with Russia through the Partnership for Peace. As a result, its mission became broader and more controversial than Cold War territorial defense.

VI A Fragile Community by 2001

Global integration spread prosperity and vulnerability together.

The new global community remained divided between those benefiting from market expansion and societies “mired in poverty and increasingly resentful.” Economic interdependence made national prosperity more sensitive to distant events, while “Balkanization” displayed the capacity of national and ethnic claims to fragment states.

The September 11 Break

The attacks of 11 September 2001 dramatically exposed the power of transnational networks and what Friedman called “super-empowered individuals.” Their capacity to exploit communications and transport technologies challenged the assumption that expanding connections would automatically produce peace.

§ Conclusion & Revision

The 1990s in three tensions

The post-Cold War decade was not simply a short-lived American triumph. It tested whether Wilsonian self-determination, market openness and collective security could coexist. The United States could facilitate agreements and interventions, yet neither commercial interdependence nor unmatched military power guaranteed equal development, national cohesion or protection from non-state violence.
