Chapter 13

The Legacy of the First World War

Study notes: The League of Nations — The Paradox of Permanent Peace

The League of Nations was a radical experiment in collective peace, but its founders remained dependent on Great Power influence and national sovereignty. Its failure to deter war obscures a lasting legacy in financial reconstruction, refugee relief, minority protection and international diplomacy.
1919,1920,1922,1923,1924,1925,1932,1939

The League of Nations, established amid the peace settlements after the First World War, promised a revolutionary alternative to two millennia of power politics. Yet its founders remained, in the account’s words, “prisoners of their diplomatic heritage.” British and French imperial calculations repeatedly competed with collective security. Its eventual inability to restrain aggression has obscured substantial work in finance, refugees, minority protection and arbitration. Lenin dismissed it as a “band of robber nations”; others called it a “toothless tiger.”

I A New Order Embedded in the Peace Treaties

The Covenant proposed collective dispute settlement while remaining tied to unequal postwar settlements.

The Covenant of the League was incorporated as the first 26 articles of the main peace treaties rather than established as an entirely independent compact. League responsibilities extended to territorial arrangements, supervised territories and later financial assistance.

The Philosophy: Peace by Conference

The old assumption that a sovereign state could legitimately use force to secure interests came under challenge. The League promised conference and arbitration instead of war, questioned the Social Darwinist assumption that the strongest states should prevail, and insisted that diplomacy could create a common security interest. This immediately raised the dilemma of how collective decisions could coexist with the foreign-policy autonomy of states.

II The Anglo-French Engine and America’s Absence

Universal aspiration, unequal Great Power control.

A League Shaped by Britain and France

The League’s membership extended beyond Europe, but its politics were dominated throughout the 1920s by London and Paris. Woodrow Wilson helped conceive the organization, but the US Senate refused to ratify the associated peace treaty. American absence weakened the global balance of the institution and left European Great Powers as its central diplomatic actors.

Mandates and the Quasi-Imperial Problem

Through the Permanent Mandates Commission (PMC), the League supervised territories removed from the defeated Central Powers. Jan Smuts helped develop mandate classifications: Class A in the Middle East (including Palestine, Iraq, Syria and Lebanon) and Classes B and C covering territories subjected to different degrees of colonial administration and oversight. Critics regarded the scheme as imperialism in new language, particularly where European powers drew boundaries using a “red crayon to a map.”

III Financial Reconstruction and Humanitarian Work

The League proved more capable in technical administration than in coercing major states.

Stabilization Loans and Monetary Reform

With support from figures such as Montagu Norman at the Bank of England, the League helped arrange reconstruction loans for vulnerable successor states and required oversight by its Financial Committee. Reconstruction loans amounted to 650 million gold crowns for Austria (1922) and more than £14 million for Hungary (1924). League-backed stabilization efforts also reached Bulgaria, Greece and Estonia. The work was presented both as reconstruction and as a means of resisting political radicalization.

Refugees and Minority Protection

The collapse of the Russian, German and Habsburg empires displaced large populations. Norwegian explorer Fridtjof Nansen, the League’s High Commissioner for Refugees, introduced the Nansen Passport (1922) to help stateless and displaced persons travel. Meanwhile, minority arrangements offered legal protection to ethnic, religious and linguistic communities. The account reports 13 states with such protections by 1932; governments often resisted them as external intrusion into national sovereignty.

The Pattern Behind Success and Failure

In smaller disputes where the Great Powers’ interests coincided, the League could achieve meaningful results. The Corfu incident exposed limits when a permanent Council member was involved: Mussolini evaded League jurisdiction and Article 16 sanctions were too vague to ensure action. The contrasting Bulgarian crisis suggested the League could perform well when Britain and France acted in unison. The organization was therefore innovative in procedure but dependent on the politics it was supposed to transcend.

§ Conclusion: An Imperfect League From an Imperfect Peace

Collective security remained hostage to sovereignty, but the League’s social and technical legacy endured.

The League could not compel the surrender of absolute sovereignty needed for robust collective security. Strong nationalism, uneven Great Power commitment and Anglo-French disputes undermined it, and it did not answer the challenge of aggressive dictators in the 1930s. Yet its efforts to stabilize economies, protect minorities, assist refugees and institutionalize international conference supplied models later adopted by the United Nations. Its failures should be studied alongside the novel practices that made it a genuine turning point in international history.
