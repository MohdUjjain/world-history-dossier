Chapter 31

Globalization

Study notes: The Synergistic Integration of the Modern World

Globalization is a historically contingent interaction of technology, markets, policy ideas and state decisions—not an autonomous or irreversible force. Integration has accelerated exchange and communications, but produced insecurities involving sovereignty, inequality, culture and financial volatility.
1776,1850,1870,1913,1914,1945,1960,1970,1971,1980,1990,1997,2000,2002

Globalization ties businesses, governments, individuals and nongovernmental organizations into increasingly extensive networks. Although the term became fashionable in the 1980s through writers such as John Naisbitt and Theodore Levitt, its deeper history includes nineteenth-century transport revolutions, interruption by war, and postwar institutions. The principal tension is between the efficient “borderless world” and unequal local consequences.

I Four Interlocking Engines

Technology alone does not explain why borders become more porous.

Why “Synergy” Matters

These forces reinforce rather than merely accompany one another: information and transport technology make global trade possible, businesses demand new rules, governments adjust standards, and policy ideas provide arguments for liberalization. The result is an expanding network of relationships—not just increased exports.

A New Corporate Outlook

Global companies increasingly identify markets by consumer demand instead of national affiliation. Cisco’s John Chambers, discussing business in Beijing, remarked that “If I wasn’t American, I would be Chinese.” The remark expresses the appeal of consumer sovereignty and a company’s worldwide commercial identity.

II The First Modern Globalization, 1850–1914

A British-led open economy appeared permanent—until it was not.

Steamships, Cables and the City of London

Coal-powered ships and railways greatly reduced journey and freight times; submarine telegraph cables allowed New York and London business information to cross the Atlantic in minutes by the 1890s. Transport costs fell markedly between 1870 and 1913. British naval power, trade and gold-standard finance sustained the system.

Keynes’s Lost World

John Maynard Keynes recalled the prewar Londoner who could order goods from distant countries while “sipping his morning tea in bed,” and invest abroad with little difficulty. More important than the conveniences was the false belief that this integration was “normal, certain, and permanent.”

III 1914–1970s: Rupture and Continuity

Wars and barriers did not stop every form of cross-border exchange.

The Political Retreat

World War I, the Great Depression, new passport and visa systems, revolutionary politics, trade restrictions and rivalry over resources checked international flows. The retreat was linked to security fears and imperial competition.

Technological and Cultural Continuities

Historians Alfred Eckes and Thomas Zeiler caution against an absolute account of “deglobalization.” Civil aviation expanded through firms such as Pan Am and Delta, while Hollywood and mass-produced Ford cars spread cultural and consumer influences internationally, despite restrictions on capital and trade.

IV The Acceleration of the Late Twentieth Century

Logistics, telecommunications and financial liberalization transformed the scale of networks.

Containerization and Satellites

Standardized containers revolutionized port handling from the 1960s; port throughput rose in the example from roughly 15 to 700 tons per hour. Intelsat IV (1971) symbolizes the communications leap. Combined with digital computing, logistics allowed geographically dispersed production and real-time information.

Deregulated Finance and Transnational Corporations

The decline of fixed exchange rates and Washington–Wall Street policies encouraged cross-border investment flows. By 2000, the figures show over 60,000 transnational corporations employing 46 million people abroad. Its cited high growth in FDI during the late 1990s reflects the speed of capital internationalization, not a guarantee of stability.

V The Ideological Contest: Advocates and Critics

The benefits and costs of globalization were politically distributed.

The “Flat World” Thesis

Thomas Friedman, especially in the discussion of The Lexus and the Olive Tree, embodies the optimistic argument: workers and firms in India and China can participate in expanding global markets, encouraging economic growth and shared standards.

The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis

Critics saw the crisis as exposing the hazards of liberalized finance. Joseph Stiglitz criticized IMF rescue programmes for prioritizing financial interests and austerity over local social conditions. In Indonesia, hardship and protests illustrated the possible political consequences of shocks transmitted through global capital markets.

VI Inevitability or Fragility?

Technical possibilities cannot substitute for political support and capable institutions.

Warnings from Earlier History

Arguments that globalization was “inevitable and irreversible” resembled the confidence of the pre-1914 world. Yet war, great-power rivalry, financial imbalances, pandemics and security threats could still disrupt flows. The US external deficit and geopolitical competition over Taiwan were among the vulnerabilities discussed.

Global Systems and Unequal Security. Global supply chains multiply efficiencies but transmit shocks. The analysis names terrorism, cyber-attacks, biological hazards and pandemic disease as potential disturbances. Durable openness thus requires strong institutions and governments willing to respond to insecurity over jobs, cultural change and unequal economic gains. Globalization is neither one technology nor a natural law. Its waves show how technology, private incentives, political ideas and government decisions combine to widen networks. The same processes create vulnerability to sudden financial reversals, war and social backlash. The crucial historical lesson is that an open global economy must be politically maintained.
