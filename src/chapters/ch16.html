Chapter 16

The Challenge to Empire in the Middle East and Asia

Study notes: The Illusion of Progress — The Challenge to Empire in Interwar Asia and the Middle East

The supposed interwar “rise of nationalism” did not simply transfer mass loyalty from empire to nation. Western-educated elites were often tied to colonial power, whereas many decisive challenges came from spontaneous popular or subaltern movements driven by taxation, hunger, injustice and repression. Nationalist histories later claimed these struggles as steps toward independence.
1918,1919,1920,1922,1925,1927,1930,1931,1935,1939

In the aftermath of the First World War, Britain and France reached their greatest territorial extents by absorbing former imperial territories through League of Nations mandates. The era is often described as a straightforward “rise of nationalism,” but the evidence in these notes suggests a more divided landscape: nationalist elites borrowed Western concepts without always rallying ordinary people, while popular groups resisted oppression through their own actions and grievances.

The investigation moves from the Paris peace settlement and its ideology of progress to India, the Middle Eastern mandates and French Indochina, ending with the question of why extensive resistance failed to dislodge European empire before 1939.

I The Post-War Mirage: Paris and Mandates

The rhetoric of self-determination coexisted with new imperial administration.

The Promise of 1919

The language of Woodrow Wilson’s national self-determination and the Bolshevik repudiation of colonialism drew nationalist delegations to the Paris Peace Conference. Yet Britain and France used German and Ottoman defeat to enlarge their authority. Under the League of Nations Mandate System, “advanced” states were meant to prepare supposedly less developed societies for self-government; in practice the arrangement often resembled colonialism under a new label.

II The “Rise of Nationalism” Reconsidered

Subaltern history emphasizes the independence of popular resistance from elite narratives.

The Conventional Story

A nationalist narrative associated with scholars such as Hans Kohn describes a Western idea of nationalism spreading “downwards” until it mobilized the masses. The notes challenge that linear picture. Elite nationalists often had economic and social connections with imperial administrators, and many preferred political argument to risky popular action.

The Subaltern Perspective

The Subaltern Studies approach focuses on the “contested, untidy nature of events.” Instead of reading every revolt as an expression of elite national planning, it asks how peasants, laborers and local communities responded to taxation, coercion and exploitation on their own terms. Later nationalist accounts could reshape failed or spontaneous uprisings into heroic stories of unified national destiny.

“To admit that colonial rule was oppressive, exploitive, and frequently brutal... required far too much honesty; it was preferable to believe that non-European peoples were simply progressing through a necessary stage of “development.””

III India: Non-Cooperation and Its Limits

Gandhi transformed political methods without immediately dismantling the Raj.

The Crisis of Imperial Legitimacy

The Amritsar Massacre (1919), in which British troops killed 379 demonstrators according to the supplied account, severely damaged the moral authority of British rule. Mohandas Gandhi responded through methods that attacked legitimacy rather than seeking a conventional battlefield victory.

Gandhi’s Language of Resistance

Satyagraha meant peaceful resistance or non-cooperation against the Raj’s moral and legal claims. Swaraj referred to “self-rule,” deliberately broad enough to unite different political groups. Hartals were organized stoppages and public protests. Gandhi’s appeal was powerful, but the coalition did not eliminate tensions among the Indian National Congress, popular Hindu identity and the Muslim League.

Institutional Limits

The Government of India Act (1935) left important functions in British hands, including powers linked in the notes to revenue, law and the Viceroy. The author stresses the persistence of imperial structures and argues that British parliamentary priorities continued to shape reform more than nationalist pressure alone.

IV Turkey, Egypt and the Arab Mandates

Different political outcomes concealed common colonial tensions.

Turkey: An Exceptional Outcome

Turkey stood apart from the British and French mandate regions, winning independence through the Turkish War of Independence (1919–22) and overturning the Treaty of Sèvres. The notes describe it as the only genuinely independent Middle Eastern case in the specific postwar comparison they develop.

Egypt: The 1919 Uprising

The Wafd, headed by Sa’d Zaghlul, pursued independence in a Wilsonian idiom. Following his exile, protests expanded spontaneously across urban lawyers and students as well as rural peasants. Their tactics included strikes, boycotts and attacks on railway and telegraph lines. Britain’s 1922 declaration of independence retained control over foreign affairs, communication and minority protection, making sovereignty severely qualified.

Iraq and Syria: Suppressed Revolts

Local resistance elsewhere confronted British and French military power. In Iraq (1920), opposition to taxation, compulsory public works and colonial authority was defeated using British aerial force; King Faysal was then installed for indirect rule. In the Syrian Great Revolt (1925–27), grievances against French demands and punishments produced widespread insurgency; Damascus was bombed and nationalist elites later pursued a collaborative National Bloc approach.

V French Indochina: Resistance and Communism

Failed formal nationalism and rural unrest opened space for communist organization.

The Colonial Economy

French rule over Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos subordinated the region to export production, especially rubber and rice. Resistance grew against economic exploitation, oppressive taxes and the government’s use of force.

From the VNQDD to the ICP and the Peasant Soviets

The Vietnamese Nationalist Party (VNQDD) backed the Yen Bay Mutiny (1930), but its suppression shattered conventional nationalist leadership. That year, Ho Chi Minh (Nguyen Ai Quoc) founded the Indochinese Communist Party (ICP). Meanwhile the Nghe An/Ha Tinh Soviet uprisings (1930–31) grew from peasant hunger, taxes and oppression. Although communists later claimed credit, the notes stress the spontaneous origins of much of the resistance; French air attacks and starvation tactics crushed it.

§ Conclusion: Why Empire Survived

Interwar protest was real and often powerful, but not a linear march to independence.

The period from 1918 to 1939 was marked by resistance without inevitable liberation. Nationalist elites frequently combined imported language with ties to imperial power; people resisted through concrete economic and social grievances; and European rulers relied on divide-and-rule methods, armored vehicles and aerial bombing to suppress revolts. Later histories could recast the failures and fractures of interwar movements as stages in a united nationalist story.

Revision essentials: the mandate system renewed empire under international language; Turkey offers the contrast of successful independence; Gandhi’s satyagraha contested Raj legitimacy while political control remained constrained; and subaltern resistance helps explain how mass grievances differed from elite-nationalist programmes.

“Nation-building... was as much about failure as it was about success... commitment to the nation competed with other more deeply rooted loyalties.”
