Chapter 32

Terrorism: September 11, 2001 and its Consequences

Study notes: The Architecture of Asymmetry — September 11 and Global Security

September 11 transformed the international response to terrorism from a predominantly criminal-law approach toward an expansive military-security framework. The growth of transnational extremist networks and the US doctrines of preemption and prevention exposed the limits of unilateral power and generated durable legal and diplomatic disputes.
1789,1967,1980,1989,1990,1998,2001,2002,2003,2004,2005,2006

The 11 September 2001 attacks showed that a powerful state could be harmed by a comparatively small, non-state organization. The resulting “War on Terror” reshaped statecraft and legal debates. The chapter examines how definitions, tactics, the al-Qaeda network, international self-defense claims and preventive-war arguments interacted with questions about American hegemony and international cooperation.

I Defining Terrorism and Its Neighbors

Analytical precision matters because the label itself is contested.

A Politically Charged Term

The account notes the lack of a universally accepted judicial definition of terrorism and invokes the saying “One man’s terrorist is another man’s freedom fighter.” These problems do not erase meaningful distinctions: targets, tactics, territorial aims and political objectives help separate terrorism from guerrilla warfare or organized crime.

“Propaganda by Deed”

Terrorist violence is meant not only to injure immediate victims but to communicate with a wider audience. It can advertise resolve, recruit supporters and provoke a state into excessive retaliation that reduces its legitimacy and increases sympathy for the perpetrators’ cause.

II Old and “New” Terrorism

A changing range of motivations and tactics altered the perceived scale of the threat.

From State Terror to Nationalist Violence

The term terror is associated with Robespierre’s French Revolutionary regime. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, revolutionary and nationalist movements also used political violence. The account mentions Bakunin, Johann Most, nationalist groups and organizations such as the IRA and ETA.

Internationalization and Mass Casualties

After 1967, Palestinian organizations carried out hijackings and attacks across borders. In the 1990s, a shift occurred toward some extremist religious or “transcendental” groups, including Aum Shinrikyo and al-Qaeda. Some sought far larger civilian death tolls and employed suicide attacks, challenging assumptions about how violent groups calculate success.

III Al-Qaeda: A Transnational Network

Transnational organization challenged territorial models of security.

Afghan Origins and Later Transformation

Osama bin Laden and Abdullah Azzam helped form al-Qaeda in the late 1980s amid mobilization for the Afghan struggle against Soviet forces. After the Soviet withdrawal, the organization developed a wider, decentralized network connecting activists and affiliated groups across multiple regions.

Programme and Claims

Its leadership presented US military presence in the Arabian Peninsula, Israel, and the restoration of a caliphate uniting the umma as core political concerns, couched in an extremist religious worldview. These were the organization’s stated goals—not representative positions of Muslim populations broadly.

Networks and Taliban Afghanistan

Al-Qaeda cooperated closely with the Taliban regime in Afghanistan, gaining sanctuary for training and coordination while affiliates elsewhere adopted elements of its ideological programme. This blurred the distinction between a state sponsor, a protected non-state network, and semi-autonomous local groups.

IV September 11: From Criminal Act to Armed Conflict

The international response recast counterterrorism as a matter of war and collective defense.

The Asymmetric Shock

The 2001 attacks used civilian aviation and international networks to strike prominent American targets. The account emphasizes the disproportion between a relatively low operational cost (approximately $500,000, by its estimate) and the devastation caused. It also gives the death toll as “over 3,000.”

Security and Collective Self-Defense

The Bush administration described the attacks as acts of war. UN Security Council Resolution 1368 condemned them, acknowledged the inherent right of self-defense, and treated terrorism as a threat to peace. NATO invoked Article 5 for the first time, interpreting the attacks as grounds for collective defense.

Afghanistan, October 2001

Operation Enduring Freedom, launched 7 October 2001, sought to remove the Taliban regime and disrupt al-Qaeda bases. It rapidly displaced the Afghan government and destroyed training infrastructure, but senior al-Qaeda figures escaped capture, illustrating the challenge of suppressing a cross-border network militarily.

V Preventive War and Iraq

The strategic doctrine extended beyond attacks already launched or imminently threatened.

Preemption versus Prevention

The 2002 National Security Strategy argued that states could not rely exclusively on traditional deterrence against networks and possible weapons of mass destruction. It treated action against emerging dangers as potentially necessary even where imminent attack was unclear.

Two forms of anticipatory force
ConceptThreat thresholdCentral question
Preemptive forceEnemy attack is imminent.Can a strike avert an impending blow?

The “Axis of Evil” and Weapons Fears

President George W. Bush described Iran, Iraq and North Korea as an “axis of evil” in 2002, arguing that WMD proliferation and extremist violence might converge. The resulting logic justified unilateral action, in the administration’s view, if international agreement could not be reached.

Iraq in 2003

Operation Iraqi Freedom was justified publicly by alleged Iraqi WMD capabilities and a broader hope of political transformation. The war proceeded without clear new UN authorization specifically endorsing the invasion, and governments including France, Russia and Germany opposed it. The later absence of the alleged stockpiles severely damaged the credibility of the rationale.

VI Legal, Political and Strategic Consequences

Operational successes coexisted with profound disputes over state power and legitimacy.

Detention and Judicial Review

Guantánamo Bay, secret “black sites”, extraordinary detention and the categorization of “unlawful combatants” prompted allegations of unlawful treatment and denial of due process. US Supreme Court decisions in 2004 and 2006 reasserted legal limits and mechanisms for reviewing detention and military commissions.

Franchising and Decentralization

The weakening of centralized al-Qaeda structures did not eliminate violent activism. The account associates attacks in Madrid (2004) and London (2005) with a more dispersed landscape of loosely linked or ideologically inspired cells.

Reputation and the Limits of Military Primacy. The failure to find Iraqi WMD stockpiles and the Abu Ghraib detainee-abuse scandal undermined American legitimacy. Long-running insurgencies illustrated that military superiority could not ensure swift political stabilization or make a unilateral order sustainable. September 11 challenged conventional concepts of state power. The US response placed self-defense, preventive force, detention, alliance obligations and international legitimacy at the center of world politics. The course of events in Afghanistan and Iraq illustrated both the reach and the limits of military superiority. The enduring debate is how to respond to transnational violent networks without abandoning legal safeguards and the cooperation necessary for durable security.
