Chapter 10

Wartime Promises and the Postwar Empires

Study notes: Wartime Promises and the Postwar Empires — The Architecting of a New Global Order

The First World War dismantled the German and Ottoman empires, but its settlement reflected a fundamental tension between wartime military promises and imperial territorial ambitions. The League of Nations mandate system reconciled these ambitions with anti-imperial rhetoric while often preserving colonial rule under another name.
1914,1915,1916,1917,1918,1919,1960

The war extended far beyond Europe. The defeat of Germany and the Ottoman Empire created a political vacuum across the Middle East, Africa and the Pacific. Secret treaties, the search for additional allies and competing versions of national self-determination shaped the new imperial map—and left disputes that would outlast the peace.

I The Middle East: Four Conflicting Promises

Britain's wartime needs pull its diplomacy in incompatible directions.

Before 1914 Britain generally defended the Ottoman Empire's integrity as a barrier to Russian expansion toward India. The Ottomans' entry into the war on the side of the Central Powers in October 1914 reversed that strategy. Britain became the leading military power operating in the Middle East and negotiated several overlapping settlements.

The Russian Carrot: Constantinople, March 1915

To retain Russia's participation in the war, Britain and France promised it the Dardanelles, Sea of Marmara, Bosphorus and Constantinople (Istanbul)—the “richest prize” of the struggle. The Constantinople Agreement proposed Russian control of the city while retaining its status as a free port. It abandoned Britain's century-old resistance to Russian control of the Straits.

The Arab Promise: Husayn–McMahon, 1915–16

Britain sought an Arab ally in Emir Husayn of Mecca. In correspondence with Sir Henry McMahon, it offered to “recognize and uphold the independence of the Arabs” across much of Arabia and the Fertile Crescent. After the Gallipoli setback, London hoped a friendly Arab state would bolster British prestige and perhaps curb French influence. Yet the letters excluded areas including Mersin and parts of Syria west of Damascus. Whether Palestine fell inside the independence promise remained deeply disputed. Interpreting references to Ottoman administrative districts, or vilayets, contributed to that controversy.

Secret Partition: Sykes–Picot, 1916

While negotiating with Husayn, Britain secretly agreed with France to divide Ottoman lands into spheres of influence. Under Sykes–Picot, France received the blue zone around Lebanon and coastal Syria; Britain claimed a red zone covering central and southern Mesopotamia. Palestine was to be internationalized to accommodate wider Christian-power interests. The agreement sat uneasily beside the rhetoric of Arab independence.

The Zionist Commitment: Balfour, November 1917

Foreign Secretary A. J. Balfour announced British support for a “national home for the Jewish people” in Palestine. Britain's strategic calculation was that backing Zionist aspirations could bolster its own claim to influence and help bypass the earlier proposal for international administration of Palestine.

II Africa: A Second Imperial Partition

A geographically brutal war ends by reallocating German possessions.

The Military and Human Reality

The African campaigns were often treated as a European-war “sideshow,” but fighting across immense distances was destructive. British, French, Belgian, South African and Portuguese forces attacked German possessions in Togoland, Cameroons, Southwest Africa and East Africa. Small mobile columns fought with machine guns amid severe disease and supply problems. In German East Africa Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck, commanding German officers and local askaris, tied down substantial Entente resources until after the European Armistice. The figures estimate that two million Africans served as soldiers or porters and approximately 200,000 died, principally from disease.

The Settlement: Transfers, Not Independence

Victorious powers took over Germany’s African possessions as League mandates. Most of Togoland, Cameroons and Tanganyika fell in Class B; Southwest Africa became a Class C mandate under South Africa. Section IV compares what these categories permitted. For African populations, the immediate change was a transfer of imperial managers rather than independence.

III The Far East: Japan's Imperial Opportunity

The German defeat in Asia and the Pacific accelerates Japanese ambition.

Occupation Across the Equator

Japan entered the war as a British ally and seized German interests in the Pacific north of the Equator—the Mariana, Caroline and Marshall islands—as well as Tsingtao (Kiaochow) in China. Australia occupied German New Guinea, and New Zealand took Samoa south of the Equator. By the end of 1914, German holdings in the Far East had been dismantled.

The Twenty-One Demands, 1915

Japan presented China with demands that would have greatly enlarged Japanese control, potentially reducing China to a protectorate in practice. Some demands were withdrawn under United States pressure, but Tokyo entrenched its position in Manchuria and Shantung.

The Racial Equality Clause, 1919

At the Paris Peace Conference, Japan proposed a racial equality clause for the Covenant of the League of Nations. Opposition led by Australia and the United States blocked the proposal. The rejection intensified Japanese resentment toward Western powers and, in the interpretation, contributed to the alienation later exploited by militarist movements.

IV The Mandate System: Annexation by Another Name

The League reconciles colonial expansion with internationalist language.

The League of Nations mandate system was a compromise between the victorious states' desire to control former enemy territories and Woodrow Wilson's anti-imperial language. It framed administration as an international duty rather than outright annexation, yet the practical outcome often resembled colonial control.

The postwar settlement re-legitimized imperial control under a new international formula. In the Middle East, overlapping British commitments and new boundaries contributed to long-running conflict. In Africa, former German possessions passed to other colonial powers and independence remained decades away. In the Far East, Japan emerged as a regional power while racial exclusion in diplomacy exposed the limits of the promised new world order.
