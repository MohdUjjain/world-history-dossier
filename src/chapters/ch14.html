Chapter 14

Why International Finance Mattered: 1919–1939

Study notes: Why International Finance Mattered — The Interwar Financial Order

The financiers who sought to restore liberal internationalism after 1918 helped create a double fragility: vulnerable cross-border payments and deteriorating political solidarity. The 1929–33 crisis did not directly cause the next war, but it undermined liberal governments and helped authoritarian expansion gain strength.
1919,1924,1925,1928,1929,1931,1932,1933

The interwar collapse is often explained chiefly through politics, yet its foundations also lay in an unstable global financial order. Bankers pursued restored trade, stable currencies and international capital flows with considerable autonomy from the governments whose security depended on their decisions. Their programme sought prosperity through liberal internationalism, but its vulnerabilities eventually separated states that needed to cooperate.

I Britain: Global Finance and the Gold Standard

Restoring London’s central position carried domestic and diplomatic costs.

The British Blueprint

After the 1918 Armistice, the City of London aimed to recover its pre-war standing in global finance. Officials and bankers linked a functioning European economy to the reintegration of Germany. Britain’s return to the gold standard in April 1925 at the pre-war rate of $4.86 per pound protected sterling’s prestige, but contributed to high interest rates, unemployment and weak industrial recovery.

The Gold Exchange Standard

Because the world faced limited gold reserves, the Bank of England and the League of Nations Financial Committee encouraged smaller countries to hold sterling or dollars as reserve assets in place of gold itself. This made financial stability dependent on confidence in a few key currencies.

German Reparations and the Ruhr

British financial appeasement favoured a solvent Germany that could pay debts and trade with Britain. London’s financial circles denounced the French and Belgian occupation of the Ruhr (1923) following German default as “madness.” The London Conference of 1924 curbed France’s independent sanctions power and moved reparations administration toward a commission in which American financial interests held a veto, as described in the account.

II The American Paradox

Wall Street internationalism collided with Washington protectionism.

By 1929, the United States occupied the centre of international trade and payments. Yet Wall Street financiers wanted overseas lending and portfolio investment, while a more isolationist Washington demanded repayments, protected domestic markets and avoided European commitments. This mismatch made the world reliant on American credit without providing an open American market through which borrowers could earn dollars.

The Fatal Loan Dependency

The United States ran a large balance-of-payments surplus, yet closed its markets to many imports. European borrowers therefore needed repeated American loans to service debt. The New York stock-market boom of 1928 pulled capital back to the US, weakening European credit before the 1929 market crash. Herbert Hoover dismissed Benjamin Strong as a “mental annex to Europe.”

III France and the Myth of Financial Imperialism

French financial policy is presented as defensive, but suspicion turned technical decisions into crises.

Defence Rather Than Expansion

British observers depicted France’s abundant capital as a weapon used to contain Germany and dominate Eastern Europe. France's financial policy also reflected defensive alliances with Poland, Romania and Yugoslavia and the substantial expense of rebuilding devastated French territory without the reparations initially promised.

The Sterling–Franc Dispute

The Bank of France accumulated substantial sterling balances while supporting the franc. Its request to exchange £20 million of sterling for gold, described here as a technical response to speculation, was interpreted in London as hostile political pressure. In 1931, leaders including Briand and Laval attempted interest-rate reductions and loans to support Britain and Germany, but mutual distrust obstructed cooperation.

IV The Global Breakdown, 1929–1933

A fragile payments network translated local setbacks into general depression.

The Great Depression was more than the Wall Street crash. Weakly regulated finance, international debts, vulnerable reserve currencies and rising tariff barriers turned downturn into a global trade and payments collapse. The financial breakdown involved Creditanstalt in Austria, British withdrawal from gold and the breakdown of reparations and war-debt cooperation.

§ Conclusion: Finance and the Fragility of Peace

A liberal programme could prove politically destructive without being a conscious conspiracy.

The financiers of the 1920s pursued an international market order without adjusting it to the new political and debt landscape left by the Great War. British attempts to build peace through trade and French efforts to purchase security through financial policy failed. The crisis of 1929–33 did not mechanically produce the Second World War, but discredited liberal governments, divided the democracies and made authoritarian expansion easier in Germany, Italy and Japan.
