Chapter 20

Stalin and the West

Study notes: Ideology, Realism, and the Path to Total War

Soviet diplomacy under Stalin married the pursuit of security, time and Western technology to the conviction that capitalism and communism were fundamentally opposed. Its tactical turns—from trade to collective security, the Nazi–Soviet Pact and the 1941 British alliance—did not remove this underlying contradiction.
1917,1922,1927,1933,1934,1935,1938,1939,1941

Under Joseph Stalin, Soviet relations with the West combined a need for Western technology, trade and strategic breathing space with a revolutionary worldview in which capitalism and socialism were ultimately antagonistic. The Soviet leadership remembered Western intervention in the Russian Civil War, feared “capitalist encirclement,” and nevertheless used capitalist expertise to construct an industrial and military state. In the 1930s, the rise of Hitler pushed Moscow between collective security and bargaining with Germany.

I The Legacy of Hostility, 1917–1927

Intervention, economic retreat and the fear of a capitalist attack shaped Soviet assumptions.

A State Born amid Encirclement

The Bolsheviks did not forget British and French assistance to the anti-Bolshevik “White” armies (1918–21). Through the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (1918), they accepted a severe territorial price to leave the First World War and preserve their government. Under Lenin, the New Economic Policy (NEP) permitted limited market activity to revive a shattered economy, while immediate international revolution was subordinated to survival.

The 1927 War Scare

The Arcos Crisis began with a British police raid on Soviet trade offices in London over suspicions of subversion. Soviet leaders treated the resulting tensions as evidence of hostile capitalist encirclement, despite the absence of clear Western invasion plans. The scare became a powerful justification for abandoning the NEP and accelerating forced industrialization.

II Revolution from Above: Trade and Subversion

Stalin sought capitalist technology to build a state that expected conflict with capitalism.

Industrialization as National Defence

Stalin’s First Five-Year Plan and forced collectivization were presented as responses to an external capitalist danger. Rapid industrial growth promised a military-industrial base able to defend Soviet power and, in revolutionary theory, to support future struggles abroad. This rationale fused domestic coercion and strategic preparation.

The Metro-Vickers and Rapallo Paradoxes

The Comintern advocated revolution against capitalist governments, but Soviet factories depended on Western engineers and equipment. In the Metro-Vickers affair (1933), six British engineers were tried on espionage and sabotage allegations, yet Soviet contracts with the firm continued. Soviet cooperation with Weimar Germany under Rapallo (1922) included secret military and economic ties; the USSR later sought naval design help from Fascist Italy, associated in the account with the destroyer Tashkent.

III Nazi Germany and Collective Security, 1933–1938

A concrete German threat led Moscow to seek diplomatic partners against fascism.

The Popular Front Turn

Hitler’s accession in 1933 transformed the threat from an abstract capitalist danger into the prospect of German attack. The Soviet Union entered the League of Nations in 1934. Foreign communist parties were instructed to temper hostility toward moderate socialists and build Popular Front anti-fascist coalitions.

The Weak Foundations of Mutual Assistance

In 1935, Moscow signed mutual-assistance agreements with France and Czechoslovakia. They had little operational substance: French anti-communism obstructed trust and detailed military arrangements were absent. Historians disagree about Soviet aims. The “realist” interpretation treats collective security as an effort to delay war; the competing “German” interpretation stresses Moscow’s willingness to bargain with Germany to divide the capitalist powers. The interpretations describe a dispute, not settled proof of a single motive.

IV Munich: The Breaking Point in 1938

Exclusion from negotiations reinforced suspicion that the West would deflect Hitler eastward.

The Munich Conference (1938) ceded the Sudetenland to Germany without Soviet participation. Stalin could suspect that Britain and France were encouraging Hitler to turn toward the USSR. Yet direct Soviet intervention faced severe practical limits: forces would need passage through Poland or Romania, neither of which trusted Soviet intentions. The Great Purges of 1936–38 had damaged the Red Army’s leadership and further weakened foreign confidence in Soviet military effectiveness.

“The Soviet Union could, with good reason, suspect that Hitler’s attentions were being directed eastwards and toward its borders.”

V The Nazi–Soviet Pact and Territorial Expansion

A deal with Germany promised time, a buffer and a European war among rival capitalist states.

Litvinov, Molotov and the Pact

In May 1939, Stalin replaced Maxim Litvinov, associated with collective security, with Vyacheslav Molotov. Anglo-French negotiations with Moscow advanced slowly while Berlin moved quickly. The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact (August 1939) enabled the partition of Poland and assigned Soviet spheres of influence in Eastern Europe. Stalin hoped to gain time for rearmament and, according to the account, to encourage a war in which Germany and the Western powers would exhaust one another.

The Buffer-Zone Argument

Moscow characterized occupation of the Baltic states and the Winter War against Finland as measures to create strategic depth. Soviet leaders feared that Finland might be used as a base for a future German assault on Leningrad. The language of defensive security accompanied territorial expansion.

VI The Icebreaker Controversy and June 1941

Plans for a future offensive must be distinguished from readiness to attack in 1941.

Two Competing Historical Readings

Defector Viktor Suvorov’s “Icebreaker” thesis argues that Stalin prepared a July 1941 strike and that Operation Barbarossa was therefore pre-emptive. Historians such as David Glantz challenge this: the Red Army was poorly organized and logistically unready for such an offensive. The source’s qualified interpretation is that Soviet planning imagined eventual offensive warfare, but Stalin’s immediate objective in June 1941 was to avoid provoking Hitler and buy more time—even while receiving warnings of invasion.

A New Alliance of Necessity

Germany invaded the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941. Moscow then entered an Anglo-Soviet alliance not because ideological differences had disappeared, but because Britain and the USSR now faced the same enemy. The Soviet state entered this struggle with a large, rapidly built industrial base but an armed force not yet prepared for the assault.

§ Conclusion: Ideology, Realism and Survival

Soviet diplomacy changed tactics without abandoning its underlying world picture.

Stalin’s western policy was dual-track: trade and technical cooperation could serve industrialization even while the leadership described capitalist conflict as ultimately unavoidable. The USSR shifted from economic stabilization to rapid rearmament, from collective security to the 1939 pact, then to an uneasy alliance with Britain after the German invasion. These were tactical responses to changing dangers within a persistent ideological framework.
