Chapter 03

Internationalism

Study notes: The Century-Long Journey Toward a World Community

Internationalism has created functional cooperation, a broader body of international law and a growing global civil society, but its ambition to transcend international anarchy remains in persistent tension with the sovereignty of nation-states.
1863,1899,1919,1945,1955,1992,2003

Internationalism is a strategy for transcending the perceived anarchy of international relations: sovereign states traditionally face no effective authority above them. In matters of common concern—peace, security and law—internationalism asks states to accept formal commitments that limit unilateral action.

Across the twentieth century, the ideal developed from narrow cross-border agreements to a wider global civil society. Yet it always confronted the same dilemma: the institutions of a world community depend on states willing to restrain their own sovereignty.

I Pragmatic Foundations: Functionalism before 1914

International organization begins with concrete problems.

International Public Unions

Expanding trade, communications and scientific cooperation made purely national solutions impractical. Governments created technical public unions to standardize common practices while preserving sovereign rights.

The Origins of Humanitarian Law

Founded in 1863, the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) introduced a non-state humanitarian presence in conflict by assisting casualties and prisoners. Its effectiveness depended on respect for the rights of governments, including the principle of “unanimous consent of the princes and nations of Europe.”

The Hague Conferences of 1899 and 1907

These conferences were among the first substantial efforts to limit the dangers of modern warfare. They did not produce disarmament, but they codified rules of land warfare and advanced peaceful dispute resolution.

Codification: The 1899 Convention on the Law and Customs of War on Land provided a general multilateral statement of land-war rules. Arbitration: The Permanent Court of Arbitration established in 1899 was a panel of nominees rather than a permanently sitting court; governments resisted compulsory arbitration.

II The League of Nations: The Great Experiment

Collective security confronts the veto of sovereign power.

The destruction of 1914–18 led to an unprecedented attempt to replace “unbridled nationalism” with collective security. Established in 1919, the League of Nations made the aspiration of organized peace a standing political institution.

“Today we stand on a bridge leading from the territorial state to the world community.”Christian Lange, 1921 Nobel Peace Prize Lecture

The Conflict over Sovereignty

The League had no standing armed forces and relied on the voluntary cooperation of its members. It remained an adjunct of Great Power politics rather than a government over states. British Foreign Secretary Lord Curzon captured the principle in 1920:

“The League does not interfere with nationality. It is upon the fact of nationhood that it rests. The League is an association of sovereign states.”Lord Curzon, 1920

Achievements and Political Eclipse

In social and technical affairs, the League coordinated efforts concerning drugs, liquor, slavery, health, communications and transit. Its legal activity included more than 120 new instruments and the creation of the Permanent Court of International Justice (PCIJ) in 1922.

Its security ambitions nonetheless collapsed into “unhappy irrelevance” in the late 1930s. It could not effectively check aggression in Manchuria, Ethiopia or the German absorption of Austria. The contrast between valuable technical cooperation and political failure is central to evaluating the League.

III The United Nations: A New Global Police?

Sovereign equality meets Great Power predominance.

Created in 1945, the United Nations inherited the League’s internationalist aspirations but sought stronger measures against “naked aggression.” Its Charter paired the language of sovereign equality with recognition of the major powers’ capacity to act independently.

The Security Council and the Veto

The five permanent members—the United States, the Soviet Union, Britain, France and China—received a veto. This embedded Great Power predominance at the center of the postwar security system even as the organization claimed universal principles.

Cold War Stalemate

The bipolar confrontation frequently immobilized the UN. The USSR and United States used the veto 174 times combined between 1946 and 1990. States therefore sought security through blocs such as NATO and the Warsaw Pact.

As newly independent countries joined in the 1960s, the UN’s agenda increasingly emphasized decolonization and economic development, extending its role beyond military security.

Legitimacy Despite Failure

The UN failed to prevent or halt atrocities in Yugoslavia and Rwanda, but it gained a distinctive legitimacy as a place where lawful international action ought to be discussed. Criticism of the 2003 Iraq War, undertaken without UN sanction, illustrated the growing expectation that powerful states justify their conduct against international legal norms.

IV Alternative Internationalisms beyond the State

Class solidarity and humanitarian intervention offer different routes.

Socialist Internationalism

The Comintern (Third International), founded in 1919, pursued a world in which international class solidarity would transcend territorial states. In practice, the organization became closely tied to Soviet power and was discredited by its instrumental role in Soviet imperial expansion.

The “New Humanitarianism”

Non-governmental organizations increasingly filled needs national governments could not or would not meet. Two humanitarian approaches differ:

Two humanitarian models
ModelUnderlying approach
ICRC (traditional)Strict neutrality to maintain access to victims and prisoners.
Médecins Sans Frontières (new)An explicitly political human-rights outlook, prepared to challenge official neutrality.

These models illustrate an important divergence: transnational assistance can operate by respecting state consent or by publicly confronting abuses. Both enlarge the sphere of actors beyond the sovereign state.

By the end of the twentieth century, political boundaries had become more permeable through international law, global organizations, professional associations, multinational companies and humanitarian networks. Yet the state remained the “foundation stone” of world organization: the very institutions designed to limit unilateral action still depended on states for consent and enforcement.

Final takeaways: The idea of an international community is now too broadly recognized to discard; NGOs, corporations and professional associations blur local and global identities; and internationalism still depends on states agreeing to limit their own freedom of action. The “bridge” toward a world community continues to be built rather than having reached its destination.
