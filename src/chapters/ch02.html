Chapter 02

Nationalism

Study notes: The Paradox of the Modern Civic Religion / The Secular Religion of the Modern Age

R. J. B. Bosworth

The nation is a modern construct and a kind of civic religion: made possible by Enlightenment rationalism, industrial modernity and mass institutions, yet sustained by invented traditions, collective myths and the exclusion of an “Other.”
1599,1789,1914,1948,2000

In 1599, Shakespeare staged a tense exchange in Henry V in which the Irish soldier MacMorris demands of a Welsh counterpart, “What ish my nation? Who talks of my nation?” The word “nationalism” had not yet emerged, yet the scene anticipates the visceral power of belonging—and its potential to divide. National identity now structures passports, taxation, civic obligations and even the sporting teams people support. It can inspire sacrifice, solidarity and violence.

The nation often appears to be an eternal fact of nature. Here it is understood as a modern political idea—one that promises collective identity but measures belonging against an excluded “Other.” That tension provides the chapter’s central problem.

I Defining the Nation: Theoretical Perspectives

What scholars mean when they call nations modern or imagined.

Scholars disagree about the nation’s origins and moral meaning. Some locate its rise in the institutions of modernity; others emphasize the fabrication of traditions, the force of shared imagination or the historical depth of ethnic identities.

For Anderson, even members of a small nation will never know most of their fellow members; nonetheless, each imagines a shared communion. For Hobsbawm, the historian must be especially alert to the deliberate construction of supposedly ancient traditions. Smith challenges accounts that minimize premodern ethnic continuities. These are contrasting arguments, not interchangeable descriptions.

II The Three Pillars of National Myth

Nature, language and history are turned into evidence of belonging.

1. Nature and “Native Soil”

Nationalists regularly present mountains, rivers, coastlines and landscapes as eternal frontiers: the Pyrenees, the Rhine or a cherished “native soil.” Distinctive flowers, foods and scenery become national emblems—tulips, kiwis or spaghetti. Yet rivers can be channeled, soils paved over and coastlines altered. Even the cultural appreciation of mountains or beaches for leisure has a history rather than timeless meaning.

The point is not that geography is unreal, but that the national meaning attached to geography is historically produced. Romanticism helped transform natural landscapes into symbols of collective identity.

2. Language and the Mother Tongue

Language is often treated as proof of a primordial nation. But there are approximately 6,800 languages with about 230 states, demonstrating that linguistic and political boundaries do not map neatly onto each other. Countries can share languages inherited through imperial histories; Canada, Paraguay and Yemen illustrate different ways languages cross supposedly natural national divisions.

Modern Hebrew, Greek and Italian are given as examples of languages deliberately revived, standardized or shaped during nation-building. Language also varies with class, gender and migration. It can be an instrument of national integration rather than an immutable precondition of statehood.

3. History and the “Usable Past”

Every nation needs a story connecting its present with an imagined past and future. Nineteenth-century writers such as Jules Michelet in France and George Bancroft in the United States helped furnish national histories with near-biblical moral purpose. The emergence of journals such as the English Historical Review, American Historical Review and Rivista Storica Italiana accompanied the professionalization of historical writing.

The “usable past” can therefore unify a population even as it distorts complexity. Hobsbawm’s critical warning is that nationalism often demands assent to what careful historical scrutiny cannot sustain. History can build nations, but rigorous history can also challenge their myths.

“Nationalism requires too much belief in what is patently not so.”Eric Hobsbawm

III The Nationalization of the Masses

How a secular political identity acquires the force of religion.

Historian George Mosse called this process the “nationalization of the masses.” In the Enlightenment and French Revolutionary imagination, popular will displaced divine right and the monarch’s personal authority. Yet as the old religious order receded, the “God of the Nation” took its place. The nation became a civic religion requiring loyalty, ritual and symbols that command something close to sacred respect.

Flags, anthems, festivals, lapel pins and sporting occasions continually rehearse belonging. These familiar rituals do not simply reflect a pre-existing nation: they help teach people to experience it as a shared moral community.

IV Bureaucracy, Railways and Industrial Modernity

Modern institutions make national identity administratively effective.

Nationalism became powerful not only because of sentiment but because it suited the practical needs of modern industrial societies. Railways—the emblem of nineteenth-century technological advance—demanded coordinated routes, administration and bounded jurisdictions. The modern bureaucracy required trained citizens who could communicate through standardized institutions.

Mass education thus joined language teaching with a state-sponsored account of national history. The resulting relationship could be expressed as a chain: industrial coordination → centralized bureaucracy → national education → standardized language and historical identity. To be modern was increasingly to be educated; to be educated was also to be “nationalized.”

V Socialism and Communism: The Anti-Nation

Why international class solidarity did not displace national loyalty.

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels offered the most influential universalist challenge: class, not ethnicity, was supposed to organize the modern world. The appeal to the workers of all countries to unite implied a solidarity that could supersede state frontiers.

The First World War exposed the fragility of that claim. In 1914, the German Social Democratic movement supported war credits for its national government, revealing national allegiance as more politically compelling than socialist internationalism. The Soviet Union likewise professed a transnational union of workers, yet Stalin, once associated with the nationality question, mobilized Russian historical heroes such as Ivan the Terrible and Peter the Great during the “Great Patriotic War.”

The recurring paradox is that an ideology designed to transcend nations repeatedly borrowed the emotional language and collective memories of the nation to survive.

VI The Jewish Experience: Exclusion, Racism and Zionism

The destructive consequences of measuring who belongs.

The experience of European Jews provides a particularly stark example of nationalism’s exclusionary logic. Enlightenment ideals initially promised individuals the chance to participate as secular citizens. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, however, racial ideologues converted the impulse to classify society into claims about immutable “blood,” making Jews the supposed ultimate outsider because they were said to lack one territory or one language within Europe.

Nationalist pseudo-science reached its most murderous form in the Nazi “Final Solution,” including mass murder at Auschwitz. A desire to classify, perfect and order society was transformed into a program of racial extermination.

Theodor Herzl proposed a Zionist state in response to antisemitic exclusion. Israel’s establishment in 1948, illustrates a different paradox: the effort to secure national refuge and sovereignty intertwines modern civic belonging with inherited religion, migration and claims of historical continuity.

VII Modern Nationalism and the Return of Religion

The late-century tension between secular civic identity and fundamentalist certainty.

At the end of the twentieth century, Francis Fukuyama’s “end of history” argument suggested the worldwide triumph of liberal democracy. Yet the sources emphasize the continuing entanglement of nationalism with religious and ideological certainty. The United States exemplifies a particular contradiction: it defines itself through liberal democracy and constant immigration, while some forms of national identity claim a distinct Christian moral foundation and cast American principles as universally valid.

The underlying pattern is the reappearance of exclusive, sacred claims within a political form born from Enlightenment rationalism. This does not imply every nationalism has the same effects; it highlights the persistent risk when belonging becomes a test of moral purity.

§ Conclusion: Nationalism’s Internal Contradiction

Four principles for revision.

Nationalism was among the most successful political forms of the period between 1789 and 2000. It accompanied major advances in education, industry, citizenship and human welfare, but it also made collective identity dependent on myth, hierarchy and exclusion. The nation began with hopes of liberty, equality and fraternity and could deploy irrational faith and violence to defend its boundaries.

Nationalism: four linked findings

The central contradiction remains: a nation promises universal progress for its members while potentially requiring the exclusion of those outside the imagined community. In this interpretation, it is simultaneously a vehicle of modernity and a source of enduring political division.
