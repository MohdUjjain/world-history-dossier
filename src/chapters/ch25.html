Chapter 25

Making the New Europe: European Integration since 1950

Study notes: The Political Economy and Institutions of European Integration

Postwar European integration did not simply replace national interest with idealistic unity. It enabled governments to exercise parts of their sovereignty collectively to restore security, prosperity and democratic legitimacy. From the Schuman Plan and Treaties of Rome to enlargement and monetary integration, the European project persisted because states repeatedly found new national purposes for shared institutions.

After the Second World War, Europe faced physical devastation, economic stagnation, the Cold War and the rapid contraction of European empires. Britain withdrew from India and Palestine, the Dutch lost their Southeast Asian empire, and France fought colonial wars in Indochina and North Africa. The difficult winter of 1946–47 highlighted economic distress, while strong communist parties in France and Italy deepened Western anxieties. The architects of European integration sought a way for diminished states to recover influence by acting together rather than pursuing ineffective national solutions alone.

I The Search for a Foundation, 1947–1950

Early cooperation created forums but could not compel coordinated decisions.

The Marshall Plan, CEEC and OEEC

The Marshall Plan (1947), or European Recovery Program, was intended to revive European economies and weaken the appeal of communism. The United States also hoped American aid would encourage a more unified Europe. Governments first assembled in the Conference on European Economic Cooperation (CEEC); the Organization for European Economic Cooperation (OEEC) followed as a permanent coordinating body. Yet the account portray the OEEC as ineffective as an engine of integration: states, especially a reluctant United Kingdom, could obstruct its initiatives and avoid binding commitments.

The Council of Europe, 1949

A French-sponsored Council of Europe offered another forum for European consultation, but it too lacked the binding authority needed to direct national policies. For subsequent integration pioneers, the obstacle was not an absence of discussion but the inability of intergovernmental organizations to make decisions that held governments to shared obligations.

II The Schuman Plan: Supranationalism Breaks Through

Pooling coal and steel offered security through shared authority.

The Declaration of 9 May 1950

French Foreign Minister Robert Schuman proposed placing French and German coal and steel production under a common authority. Jean Monnet was the scheme’s principal planner; Schuman was its public political sponsor. Two innovations mattered: supranationalism, in which accepted joint rules could bind participating states, and the deliberate decision to build around Franco-German reconciliation, even without Britain.

The Six and the ECSC

The European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) united France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg. This core group of Six endured until the first enlargement in 1973. Coal and steel were strategically chosen: they were vital to industrial recovery and to the production of weapons. Common institutions offered a way to make German economic rehabilitation compatible with French security.

The Failure of the European Defence Community

The proposed European Defence Community (EDC) attempted to combine the armed forces of the Six so that West German rearmament would not require a fully independent German army. The French National Assembly rejected ratification in 1954. This failure checked supranational ambitions in defence, while the more conventional Western European Union offered an alternative route for Western security cooperation.

III Rome: Economic Integration and the Institutional Quartet

The Six rebuilt momentum around common markets and shared rule-making.

Messina and the Treaties of Rome

At Messina (1955), the Six resumed negotiations, leading to the Treaties of Rome (1957). These established Euratom for atomic-energy cooperation and the European Economic Community (EEC). The EEC aimed to remove internal customs tariffs, establish a common external tariff and develop common policies, notably the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP).

The institutions blended supranational and national power. Although a central Commission could promote integration, the Council gave ministers from member states a decisive place in lawmaking. This balance between common authority and governmental control became one of the project’s defining features.

IV Why Integrate? Three Interpretive Traditions

Peace ideals, state interests and economic necessities overlapped.

The Traditional Narrative of Peace

Early proponents such as Monnet and Paul-Henri Spaak emphasized the desire to make another European war impossible through durable cooperation. The experience of 1914 and 1939 gave the peace argument emotional force. But a purely heroic narrative can obscure the specific national calculations behind the institutions.

The Revisionist and Realist Argument

Modern interpretations place greater emphasis on national political interest. France used integration to take the initiative in European diplomacy and manage the German problem; West Germany sought acceptance after the war, reconciliation with France and secure attachment to the Western camp; Italy could regain standing without appearing threatening. Collective institutions allowed states to regain an international role that isolated action could not provide.

Alan Milward: Rescue of the Nation-State

Economic historian Alan Milward described integration as the “European rescue of the nation-state.” Democratic governments required growth, employment and credible economic policy to retain electoral legitimacy. French access to German coke, anxieties about German steel competition and Dutch fears of a return to 1930s-style protectionism all shaped the settlement. The Dutch advocate Jan-Willem Beyen pressed for a customs union that suited a small economy dependent on open markets.

V Commission versus Council: Who Exercised Power?

The central executive’s original promise gave way to increasingly assertive national governments.

Proposal and Decision

The ECSC’s High Authority had embodied an ambitious central executive. Under the Treaty of Rome, the Commission retained the initiative in proposing common rules, but the Council of Ministers made the binding decisions. The institutional rule is summarized as “the Commission proposes; the Council disposes.” Even figures sometimes called “Mr Europe,” such as Monnet and Walter Hallstein, therefore operated in a system increasingly shaped by national ministers.

The Empty Chair and Luxembourg, 1966

French President Charles de Gaulle challenged moves toward majority voting. The resulting empty-chair crisis and Luxembourg Compromise (1966) strengthened the practice of seeking unanimity when a government claimed a question involved its “vital interest.” The accord narrowed the scope for overruling states and underscored the continuing power of the national executive.

The European Council, from 1975

The regular meetings of European heads of state and government from 1975 further elevated national political leadership. Prime ministers and presidents could give strategic direction to integration while ministers worked through the detailed legislation. The resulting system was not a simple transfer of sovereignty from capitals to Brussels; it was a constantly negotiated collective exercise of state power.

VI Deepening and Widening: The Agenda Expands

New members and new policy problems supplied fresh reasons to cooperate.

From Economic Community to Monetary Union

Cooperation spread beyond tariffs and industrial sectors. Cross-border pollution created an argument for environmental policy; the weakening of the Bretton Woods monetary system encouraged exchange-rate coordination and the European Monetary System. Integration ultimately advanced toward Economic and Monetary Union (EMU) and the euro, launched in physical currency form in 2002 in the account’s chronology.

Successive Rounds of Enlargement

Enlargement made the European project broader in geography and policy. The United Kingdom, Ireland and Denmark joined in 1973. Greece (1981), then Spain and Portugal (1986), connected membership to democratic consolidation after authoritarian rule. Austria, Finland and Sweden joined in 1995; the large 2004 expansion brought ten additional states, many from Central and Eastern Europe.

Does Widening Undermine Deepening?

Observers feared that bringing more states into the Community would impede closer union. The account argues the relationship was not so simple: poorer entrants such as Ireland, Greece and Spain reinforced demands for structural and cohesion funds, while wealthy northern states such as Sweden and Finland preferred participation to becoming a “mailbox democracy” obliged to follow Brussels rules without helping make them. More members could therefore create new rationales for deeper common policies.

§ Conclusion: The Resilience of a Shared Sovereignty

The Union survived because cooperation remained useful to its member governments.

European integration emerged from the insecurity and economic weakness of postwar Europe and persisted because each generation discovered new tasks—industrial recovery, trade, agricultural policy, economic stability, regional redistribution, democracy and the environment—that were difficult to solve alone. Its enduring paradox is that collective institutions could strengthen the nation-state rather than simply abolish or supersede it.
