Chapter 18

The Challenge in Europe, 1935–1941

Study notes: The Architects of Ruin — The Collapse of the European Order, 1935–1941

The European order established in 1919–20 collapsed under a dynamic, ideologically driven challenge led by Nazi Germany. Revisionist powers exploited the structural fragility, demographic anxieties and diplomatic disunity of the states charged with defending Versailles. The outcome was not treaty adjustment but the destruction of the European status quo.
1919,1935,1936,1938,1939,1940,1941

Between 1935 and 1941, Europe’s international system underwent a transformation tantamount to the “utter destruction” of the old continental balance. The principal division lay between the challengers—powers seeking to revise the settlement through ideology and force—and the challenged—states whose security rested on the post-1918 order but whose capacity and will to defend it increasingly diverged.

I The Anatomy of the Challenge

Revisionism ranged from unlimited German ambition to localized frontier claims.

The Versailles settlement depended on the victory of the Allied powers in 1918. By the mid-1930s, the states dissatisfied with that order posed different degrees of challenge. Britain and France were its principal guardians; Poland, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia owed much of their international position—and in this interpretation even their very existence—to its territorial settlements. For such states, border revision was often an existential threat, not a minor concession.

II Germany: Treaty Revision and Radical Ideology

A widely shared anti-Versailles grievance intersected with Hitler’s far broader programme.

The Overthrow of Versailles

Many Germans rejected the 1918 defeat through the “stab-in-the-back” narrative. Opposition centered on territorial losses to Poland, the treaty’s 100,000-man army ceiling and prohibition of a military air force, and the war-guilt and reparations provisions. These grievances reached beyond committed Nazis: they resonated within German public and elite opinion.

Beyond Reparation: Hitler’s World Picture

Hitler’s goals extended far beyond revising an unpopular peace. The account emphasizes antisemitism, race as the supposed motor of history, perpetual Social Darwinist struggle and Lebensraum in Eastern Europe at the expense of the Soviet Union. Its intentionalist interpretation treats Hitler’s power and expansionist aims as real, deliberate and long-standing—not a mere product of diplomatic accidents.

The Economic Impulse

German rearmament created compounding strains on supplies of steel, aluminium and oil. The Four-Year Plan (1936) sought to ready industry and the armed forces for war. In the account’s interpretation, territorial conquest became a means of acquiring resources for further military expansion: an aggressive feedback loop between war preparedness and resource needs.

III The Italian and Soviet Dimensions

Other revisionist powers reshaped the strategic environment but followed distinct paths.

Mussolini and the “Mediterranean Prison”

Benito Mussolini believed Italy’s ambitions were constrained by British and French control of Gibraltar, Malta, Cyprus and Suez. He celebrated war as a forge of national destiny and pursued dominance in the Adriatic, Albania, Yugoslavia and Ethiopia. Unable to confront Britain and France unaided, he joined Hitler in the Rome–Berlin Axis (1936) and later the Pact of Steel (1939).

Stalin’s Revisionist Pivot

The Soviet Union initially approached the 1930s as a relatively status-quo power, preoccupied with internal upheaval and the purges. In 1939, the Nazi–Soviet Pact enabled Stalin to pursue territorial recovery in Poland, the Baltic region and Romania. The account emphasizes the paradox of a state previously seeking security becoming an active participant in dismantling the territorial settlement.

IV The Crisis of the Status Quo

The powers committed to Versailles were divided by their own political and strategic weaknesses.

France: The “Hollow Years”

French security was shaped by the human cost of the First World War. During the “hollow years” (1936–39), the number of young men reaching military age was sharply reduced by the wartime birth deficit. France had relied on a demilitarized Rhineland as an open approach to Germany. Its remilitarization undermined that strategy. The account argues that after the Ruhr occupation of 1923, France increasingly lacked the political will to enforce Versailles through force.

Britain: Imperial Responsibilities and Revisionist Sympathy

Britain saw itself as a global imperial power and often treated continental disputes as only one among several theatres of concern. Many Britons viewed Versailles as excessively severe and German demands for change as partly legitimate. A strong commitment to the security of Europe emerged slowly, after several components of the postwar arrangement had already collapsed.

§ Conclusion: The Triumph of Dynamism

The old balance collapsed because its defenders could not match a determined ideological challenge.

By June 1940, Germany dominated much of Europe, achieving in weeks what it had failed to achieve in years during the First World War. The explanation does not attribute this solely to superiority in troop or tank numbers. It emphasizes speed, ruthlessness, operational dynamism and ideological conviction, which repeatedly left Germany’s opponents unable to respond effectively. The striking asymmetry was political as well as military: challengers used surprise, audacity and unorthodox diplomacy; defenders relied on diplomatic protest, negotiation and delayed mobilization. Success invited ever bolder demands until the status quo was destroyed.

Revision essentials: distinguish legitimate treaty grievances from Hitler’s far more expansive ideological programme; connect Italian and Soviet revisionism to German success; explain how French demography and strategy and British global commitments weakened resistance; and trace the escalation from 1935 rearmament to Barbarossa in 1941. The lesson is that a settlement cannot survive if its founders lose the collective will to defend it.
