Chapter 22

The Grand Alliance, 1941–1945

Study notes: Personal Diplomacy and the Architecture of Victory (1941–1945)

The Grand Alliance united Britain, the Soviet Union and the United States not by a common ideology but by the necessity of defeating the Axis. Germany First, unconditional surrender and personal diplomacy kept it together. Yet disagreements over strategy, self-determination, Poland and spheres of influence made the wartime victory a prelude to Cold War tension.
1941,1942,1943,1944,1945

The wartime coalition that Churchill called the Grand Alliance was an uneasy partnership between Winston Churchill, Franklin D. Roosevelt and Josef Stalin. Some forty-five governments ultimately participated in the Allied cause, but the principal decisions lay with the Big Three. Their political systems and postwar expectations diverged even as their common enemy made cooperation indispensable.

I Foundations of a Fragile Unity

Shared survival required commitments without a comprehensive formal alliance treaty.

Principles of Wartime Cooperation

In August 1941, Roosevelt and Churchill drafted the Atlantic Charter, setting out aspirations for self-determination, free trade and a different international order. Churchill called it “not a law, but a star”; Britain’s imperial priorities and Soviet security concerns made its ideals contested from the outset. The Declaration by United Nations (January 1942) committed the signatories to use their resources against the Axis and not to conclude a separate peace.

The Germany-First Commitment

The ABC-1 talks (early 1941) established the priority of defeating Germany before Japan, a policy that became the sine qua non of cooperation. It reassured Moscow that the United States and Britain would devote the main weight of their power to the European war. An agreement on military priority did not erase the different visions of the peace that would follow.

II The Strategic Friction over a Second Front

Military plans were inseparable from the politics of reassuring Stalin.

The Political Question

Soviet Foreign Minister V. M. Molotov argued that the western Second Front was a matter for “statesmen, not generals.” For Stalin, a delay in opening a direct front in France threatened Soviet survival and suggested possible Western bad faith. Roosevelt wanted to sustain Stalin’s confidence, while operational planners debated whether the Allies were ready for a cross-Channel landing.

The Mediterranean Diversion

Churchill persuaded Roosevelt to undertake Operation TORCH (North Africa, 1942) rather than a premature invasion of France. With a “crocodile” analogy, he favoured attacking the Mediterranean “soft underbelly” while preparing for an assault on northern Europe. His preference for peripheral attrition, coloured by First World War memories, was followed by Allied landings in Sicily and Italy (1943). Stalin accepted these campaigns but insisted that they were no substitute for 50–60 divisions fighting in France.

III Teheran: The Apogee of Cooperation

In November 1943 the Big Three made their most consequential shared military commitments.

OVERLORD and the Shape of Europe

At the Teheran Conference (November 1943), the leaders committed to Operation OVERLORD, the major cross-Channel landing planned for May 1944. It closed off Churchill’s hopes of making the Mediterranean the principal path to victory. Political bargains were equally significant: Churchill demonstrated a westward shift of Poland’s borders with matchsticks, envisioning compensation in former German territory for Soviet gains in the east.

Roosevelt’s Four Policemen

Roosevelt proposed that the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union and China act as “Four Policemen” to preserve peace. This imagined a postwar security system in which smaller states would put trust in Great Power leadership. The scheme reveals how the promise of collective internationalism could coexist with explicit recognition of unequal international power.

IV Poland and the Balkan Percentages

The meaning of liberation exposed incompatible interpretations of sovereignty.

The Polish Litmus Test

As Allied armies advanced into Europe, Poland became a measure of whether the alliance could survive peace. For London and Washington, self-determination implied genuine national independence and representative government. Stalin required a “friendly” government on the Soviet frontier and interpreted security through political control of neighbouring states. The physical fact of military occupation increasingly shaped the settlement more than wartime declarations.

The Percentages Agreement (October 1944)

Churchill and Stalin discussed approximate Balkan spheres of influence: Greece, 90 per cent British; Romania and Bulgaria, 90 per cent Soviet; and Yugoslavia and Hungary, roughly 50–50. The proposal sought to contain rival interventions through explicit understandings, but ignored the popular sovereignty proclaimed in the Atlantic Charter. Poland was not included, although its proposed border shift was already being discussed.

V Yalta and the Breakdown of Wartime Trust

A declaration of political freedom sat uneasily beside Soviet military power.

The Declaration on Liberated Europe

At Yalta (February 1945), the last meeting of the original Big Three, the leaders endorsed a Declaration on Liberated Europe. Its promises of political choice raised expectations that could not easily be reconciled with Soviet control in Eastern Europe. In Poland, Stalin supported the Lublin-based government, resisting demands for a genuinely inclusive administration.

Leadership and Nationalism after Victory

The death of Roosevelt in April 1945 and succession of Harry Truman were followed by a more confrontational American style. The wartime modus vivendi became increasingly difficult to sustain. Stalin anticipated that liberated countries would remain intensely nationalistic, potentially challenging both the European empires and Soviet spheres of influence. The Grand Alliance had not resolved the relationship between Great Power direction and national choice.

§ Conclusion: Victory and the Seeds of Bipolarity

The alliance achieved military victory but never became a lasting union.

Unconditional surrender, announced at Casablanca (1943), was intended to prevent a separate peace and make the political remaking of defeated Germany possible. The European Advisory Commission (EAC) drew up occupation arrangements that would endure far beyond their supposedly temporary wartime purpose. Anglo-American cooperation rested on shared institutions, intelligence and language but was strained by disputes over British imperial preference and Washington’s free-trade agenda; the Soviet–Western bond endured only so long as the defeat of Hitler was overriding.

The Grand Alliance was a marriage of necessity, not a permanent peace settlement. Its decisive achievements were Germany First, commitment to unconditional surrender and coordinated operations culminating in OVERLORD. Yet the Atlantic Charter’s principles collided with imperial preferences, Soviet security zones and rival concepts of liberation. Its legacy included Great Power cooperation in the United Nations Security Council and the beginnings of the Cold War.
