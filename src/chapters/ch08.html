Chapter 08

The July Crisis

Study notes: The July Crisis — A Perfect Storm of Statecraft

The outbreak of the First World War was an advertent event: not a mere accident of alliance momentum. Nationalism, rivalries and military plans created pressure, but individual statesmen in Vienna, Berlin, St. Petersburg, Paris and London chose to treat war as a defensible instrument of honour, security and national standing.
1911,1912,1913,1914

The five weeks between the Sarajevo assassination (28 June 1914) and Britain’s declaration of war (4 August) were marked by calculated decisions, misread signals and increasingly rigid military requirements. To understand the “perfect storm,” distinguish its long-term fuel from the decisions that ignited it.

I The Fracturing Balance, 1911–1913

Diplomatic confrontations and Balkan wars harden European expectations.

Agadir Crisis, 1911

Germany’s effort to obstruct French control of Morocco failed, reinforcing French resolve. General Joseph Joffre became French chief of staff with greater power and developed an offensive plan; Raymond Poincaré strengthened ties to Russia and Britain. Germany, worried that its army could not match the Franco-Russian combination, added 136,000 troops to its forces.

Balkan Wars and a Rising Serbia

Ottoman weakness encouraged the Balkan League—Serbia, Greece, Montenegro and Bulgaria—with Russian support. Serbian territorial success alarmed Austria-Hungary. Foreign Minister Count Leopold Berchtold helped deny Serbia an Adriatic outlet through the creation of Albania. Across four confrontations with Serbia and Montenegro in 1912–13, Vienna’s coercive “militant diplomacy” appeared effective, encouraging the belief that threats or force could settle the Serbian question.

II Sarajevo and the Black Hand

A regional assassination becomes an imperial test of strength.

Serbian politics were divided between the civilian government of Nikola Pašić and a military intelligence network led by Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijević (“Apis”). Apis headed the Black Hand, a clandestine Greater Serbian nationalist organization.

The Attack, 28 June 1914

Three Bosnian Serb students—Gavrilo Princip, Trifko Grabež and Nedeljko Čabrinović—were armed with bombs and Browning pistols and smuggled into Bosnia through the Black Hand network. During Archduke Franz Ferdinand’s Sarajevo visit, an initial bomb attack failed. A mistake in the car’s movements then brought the archduke and his wife Sophie directly before Princip, who shot and killed them.

“Because of their deaths, a war would come that eventually claimed 9.5 million more combatants’ lives.”

III Five Phases of the July Crisis

From punishment of Serbia to European general war.

The July Crisis: five-stage escalation
Phase / datesDecision or development
1 · Punish Serbia
28 June–7 July		Vienna chooses confrontation. 5–6 July: Kaiser Wilhelm II and Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg give Austria-Hungary the German “blank cheque” of support.
2 · The lull
8–18 July		Berlin and Vienna seek an apparently calm interval before a localized action. Leaks through Italy alert Russia and Serbia to their intentions.
3 · Ultimatum prepared
19–23 July		Vienna finalizes a 48-hour ultimatum intended to be unacceptable, timing its delivery after Poincaré leaves St. Petersburg.
4 · Rupture
23–28 July		Serbia accepts much of the demand but rejects a joint investigation it considers incompatible with sovereignty. Austria-Hungary breaks relations and declares war 28 July.
5 · General war
28 July–4 August		Russia orders general mobilization 30 July; Germany enacts its western offensive through neutral Belgium; Britain enters war 4 August.

Germany regarded Russian general mobilization as a casus belli. Its Schlieffen–Moltke strategy envisaged defeating France rapidly through neutral Belgium before concentrating on Russia. The violation of Belgian neutrality unified the previously divided British cabinet behind intervention. These decisions turned an Austro-Serbian confrontation into a continental war.

IV The Anatomy of Escalation

Civilian aims collide with inflexible military timetables.

Austria-Hungary: The Harvest Leave Delay

General Conrad von Hötzendorf had released troops for harvest leave; the monarchy could not launch its desired offensive immediately. By the time action became possible, much of the sympathy initially generated by the assassination had evaporated.

Conflicting Deployment Plans

Conrad initially implemented “Plan B” against Serbia even while Russia was mobilizing. He then had to transfer forces in a chaotic and lengthy redeployment to the Russian frontier. Germany’s planned western strike, meanwhile, required speed and near-perfect execution: its underlying assumptions treated delay as existential danger and pushed political leaders toward rapid escalation.

V Historiography: Why War Happened

From the Versailles verdict to a focus on intentional choice.

Versailles (1919) primarily blamed Germany and Austria-Hungary. Revisionists argued that Europe “slithered” into unintended war without a single wholly responsible government. Fritz Fischer (1960s) portrayed German policy as a preventive-war calculation amid domestic and international pressures. The modern interpretation in these notes emphasizes advertent decisions: leaders separately judged war acceptable in defence of honour and security.

§ Conclusion: The Convergence of Choice

War as a deliberate option—not an act of nature.

The July Crisis brought together a succession of decisions by men who believed military action could protect honour, alliances or security. Opportunities for restraint were repeatedly neglected as preserving strategic standing took priority over preserving peace. This “perfect storm” was ultimately an act of statecraft. The governments did not share identical goals, but their choices allowed a local crisis to become European war.
