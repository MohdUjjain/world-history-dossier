Chapter 08

The July Crisis

Study notes: The July Crisis — Choices and Consequences

Samuel R. Williamson Jr.

How did Austria-Hungary's confrontation with Serbia become a European war? Williamson traces five stages in which Vienna and Berlin bore the leading responsibility, yet decisions elsewhere also mattered. War was advertent: leaders accepted and escalated risks rather than being helplessly pulled along by treaty machinery.
1911,1912,1913,1914,1915,1919,1920

Emperor Franz Joseph signed a declaration of war on Serbia on 28 July 1914, exactly a month after the Sarajevo assassinations. Almost all Europe's Great Powers were at war within ten days. Samuel R. Williamson Jr. asks why senior statesmen accepted decisions that steadily removed opportunities for restraint. Long-term crises supplied the pressure, but neither geography nor alliances independently forced the catastrophe.

I Europe's Worsening Position, 1911–1913

Successive confrontations made war increasingly thinkable.

Morocco, armies and perceived encirclement

The of 1911 ended with France gaining Morocco and Germany a slice of French Congo. French politicians celebrated; General Joseph Joffre became chief of staff and developed offensive planning, and Raymond Poincaré later strengthened ties to Russia and Britain and backed France's three-year-service law. Italy used Ottoman vulnerability to invade Libya. Berlin concluded that its land army was falling behind the Franco-Russian combination and added 136,000 troops by 1914; France and Russia increased forces in response. The German naval race with Britain eased somewhat, but German fears of strategic “encirclement” deepened.

The Balkan Wars: an unsettling lesson in coercion

The formed in 1912 with Russian encouragement. Montenegro attacked Ottoman positions in October; its partners, including Serbia, rapidly gained territory and prestige. Habsburg foreign minister Count Leopold Berchtold promoted independent Albania to deny Serbia an Adriatic outlet. Vienna contemplated force against Serbia in 1912, Montenegro in 1913, and Serbia again that October, when Belgrade complied with a seven-day ultimatum. These episodes appeared to validate : threaten neighbours until they retreat. But Russia had troops close to the Habsburg frontier and could intervene in another Serbian crisis.

Vienna's international anxieties were tied to domestic struggles. Czechs, Romanians and challenged the multiethnic empire; Hungary's Premier István Tisza feared adding further Slavic subjects. Italy quarrelled with Vienna over Albania, and Romania drifted toward Russia. Germany worried about the Liman von Sanders dispute involving Ottoman forces, an improving Russian military and Anglo-French or Anglo-Russian staff conversations. Nor was the Entente stable: Ireland distracted Britain, socialists challenged French military policy, and Russian strikes increased. Neither alliance was a single, disciplined bloc.

II Sarajevo: A Plot and a State Divided

Secret military networks must not be confused with civilian authorization.

The Balkan victories enlarged Serbia and brought new clashes between its elected leadership and military intelligence. Prime Minister Nikola Pašić wanted civilian control; Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijević, nicknamed , headed intelligence and helped found the , a clandestine Greater Serbian movement. When Gavrilo Princip, Trifko Grabež and Nedeljko Čabrinović proposed assassinating Franz Ferdinand, Apis arranged weapons and their smuggling into Bosnia. Williamson treats the Black Hand network and official Serbian government as different political actors.

How much did Belgrade know?

By early June Interior Minister Stefan Protić warned Pašić about the plot. Pašić authorized attempts to stop the conspirators and demanded an army explanation, while Apis concealed what his organization had done. Some Serbian ministers were aware of the danger before the archduke's visit, but the author finds no hard evidence of an official warning to Vienna. Whether Russian attaché Victor Artamonov knew remains uncertain. That evidence does not justify a simple claim that the entire Serbian cabinet ordered the murder.

28 June: an accidental encounter, not an automatic war

The archduke and his wife Sophie travelled through Sarajevo during the Serbian commemoration of Kosovo in 1389. An assassin's bomb missed its intended effect. Later, General Oskar Potiorek failed to inform the driver that the procession had changed route. When the car stopped to reverse, Princip shot the couple. The wrong turn was accidental; the subsequent transformation of the deaths into a European war involved new decisions in five stages.

III Vienna, Berlin and the Ultimatum, 28 June–23 July

The first three stages converted outrage into an intentional showdown.

Phase 1 · 28 June–7 July: the choice to punish Serbia

By 3 July, leading Habsburg officials wanted Serbia held responsible. General Conrad von Hötzendorf and several ministers favoured war; Tisza first demanded a diplomatic confrontation. Vienna reached this position without pressure from Berlin, but sought its ally's support to deter Russia. Count Alexander Hoyos brought the Habsburg case to Kaiser Wilhelm II, who on 5 July and with Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg on 6 July issued the . Germany promised support without knowing exactly what Vienna would demand. Historians disagree whether Berlin meant merely to help its ally or encourage a larger preventive war; Williamson says the precise Habsburg proposal was not known to Berlin beforehand.

At the Austro-Hungarian of 7 July, Tisza resisted immediate war. Conrad explained that kept troops away until about 21–22 July. The delay gave Berchtold time to build agreement and seek evidence linking the plot to Belgrade. It also eroded the opportunity to act while European sympathy was fresh and placed the ultimatum near a French presidential visit to Russia. German leaders expected haste, but important generals and diplomats remained on holiday.

Phase 2 · 8–18 July: diplomacy that looked deceptively calm

Berlin and Vienna tried to lull the other powers. Vienna's financial markets steadied, Wilhelm continued his North Sea cruise and Conrad went hiking. But German ambassador Hans von Flotow disclosed Habsburg intentions to Italy's foreign minister San Giuliano; his subsequent telegrams warned several capitals. Habsburg codebreakers detected the leaks. Thus Russia and Serbia expected demands before they were delivered. London's Irish crisis and the Paris Caillaux trial helped distract public attention, but the secrecy strategy was never fully successful.

Phase 3 · 19–23 July: the demand was designed to be refused

On 19 July, Vienna approved the ultimatum and agreed with Tisza that any territorial acquisition from Serbia should be minor. At 6 p.m. on 23 July it delivered the after Poincaré had departed St Petersburg. One demand required cooperation with Habsburg investigators inside Serbia. Williamson judges the terms were formulated for rejection because Vienna sought a military confrontation. British foreign secretary Edward Grey warned against precipitate steps, but the clock had been deliberately set running.

IV From Ultimatum to General War, 23 July–4 August

The last two stages show diplomacy, mobilization and war plans intersecting.

Phase 4 · 23–28 July: the reply and the first military measures

Serbia offered a conciliatory answer but resisted the joint murder investigation as an infringement on . Habsburg envoy Wladimir Giesl rejected it at 6 p.m. on 25 July, broke relations and departed; Serbia began mobilizing. Russia did not order Belgrade to defy the ultimatum. Sazonov nevertheless resolved to support Serbia, discussing from 24 July. That afternoon Russia's military command ordered troop recalls in Odessa, Moscow, Kiev and Kazan. German intelligence noticed. Russia therefore took preliminary military steps before Vienna declared war, but these should not be confused with the nationwide order six days later.

On 25 July Russian generals pressed for , easier administratively and more threatening to Germany. France's ambassador Maurice Paléologue did not urge Russian restraint. Franz Joseph authorized steps against Serbia; meanwhile German ministers and army officers sent conflicting advice—Bethmann explored caution as Moltke urged rapid military action. Berchtold resolved the contradiction for Vienna: Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on 28 July. The chance to confine the dispute to a diplomatic ultimatum was now sharply reduced.

Phase 5 · 28 July–4 August: decisions under military pressure

Shots exchanged near Belgrade during the night of 28–29 July produced exaggerated reports that pushed Russian generals toward a full order. Tsar Nicholas II first accepted mobilization orders on 29 July, then withdrew them following Wilhelm's personal telegrams. At 4 p.m. on 30 July, under renewed pressure, Nicholas authorized general mobilization: the first Great Power nationwide order. Berlin treated this as a , but Bethmann waited until 31 July for confirmation so that Russia would appear the aggressor. Germany then issued ultimatums and began its own mobilization on 1 August.

The costs of military plans

Conrad ordered on 30 July, sending essential forces toward Serbia even though war with Russia was looming. His later switch to required circuitous, delayed redeployment from the Balkans to the Russian frontier. Germany's assumed a rapid strike through neutral Belgium to defeat France before turning east. Seizing Liège was essential to its timetable, and Russia's growing mobilization speed made Berlin more impatient. These were not independent machines: leaders had authorized plans that made political postponement increasingly difficult.

France and Britain faced very different political constraints

Poincaré and Premier René Viviani reached Paris on 29 July. To avoid appearing aggressive and improve the chance of British help, Poincaré ordered French troops to remain 10 kilometres behind the German border until 2 August. In Britain the Irish issue delayed serious cabinet debate; Grey tried mediation while French ambassador Paul Cambon sought a pledge. Naval precautions did go ahead. By 2 August, German threats to France's Channel coast, a letter of support from Conservative leaders and transformed the argument. Threatened Belgian territory gave divided ministers a shared public ground for intervention.

German forces violated Belgium's neutrality and rejected Britain's demand to stop. Britain declared war on 4 August at 11 p.m., while plans for the British Expeditionary Force were advancing. Italy, despite membership of the Triple Alliance, declared neutrality on 2 August: Vienna had refused compensation such as Trentino, and Italian opinion opposed helping Austria-Hungary. Italy eventually fought alongside the Entente in 1915. Alliance ties did not produce identical or automatic decisions.

V Historians and Responsibility

Deliberate choices need not imply that all governments were equally guilty.

The Versailles peace settlement of 1919–20 assigned principal blame to Germany and Austria-Hungary. German historians and governments disputed that verdict and selectively released records; other countries followed with their own diplomatic documents. The questioned exclusive responsibility; another argument, associated with Lloyd George's image of states “slithering” into war, emphasized unintended escalation. Structural interpretations stressed nationalism, militarism, arms races, alliances and imperial tensions as forces greater than any single leader.

In the 1960s stressed the centrality of German decisions and argued that preventive-war calculations may have shaped Berlin's policy. Williamson accepts the paramount role of Vienna and Berlin, but includes Serbia's compromised military-civilian relationship, Russia's early military actions and 30 July mobilization, France's failure to restrain Russia, and British hesitation. These are important contributions, not equivalent shares of guilt.

For Williamson, the war was : statesmen judged military action an acceptable response to threats to national , status and alliances. Long-standing pressures shaped what they saw, yet the decisive acts remained choices that could have been made differently. A historical explanation must therefore distinguish underlying conditions, immediate triggers, identifiable decisions and unequal responsibility.
